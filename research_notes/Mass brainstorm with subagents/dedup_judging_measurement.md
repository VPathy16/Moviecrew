# From a pile of LLM-generated ideas to a trustworthy shortlist: deduplication, grouping, judging, ranking and measurement

Notes compiled 19 November 2026. Each finding carries its year. Findings from 2023 or earlier are marked "older"; judge-model findings from 2023 and 2024 concern models (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Claude 3.5) that are now several generations old. "Verified" means I read the number in the paper text or abstract; "secondary" means it came from a search summary, press piece or vendor page and should be checked before it is quoted.

## 1. Deduplication and grouping

### Takeaway
The common recipe is sentence embeddings plus a cosine-similarity cut-off near 0.8, but that cut-off is set by eye in every source I found and does not transfer between embedding models. Embeddings alone split true rewordings apart and treat negated or opposite statements as near-identical, so the best-performing designs use embeddings only to find candidate matches and let an LLM (or a person) decide "same idea or not".

### Cited Findings

#### Embedding similarity with a threshold
- (2024, verified) Si, Yang and Hashimoto embedded 4,000 LLM-generated seed ideas per topic with all-MiniLM-L6-v2 and called two ideas duplicates at cosine similarity 0.8 or more. Only about 200 of 4,000 (5%) were non-duplicates, and the count of new unique ideas flattened as more were generated, even though the generator's prompt carried the titles of all previously generated ideas with an instruction to avoid repeating them. The threshold was set "based on manual inspection". The paper does not report how long the seed ideas were. — [Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109)
- (2024, verified) Meincke, Mollick and Terwiesch used Google's Universal Sentence Encoder and also treated similarity above 0.8 as "an identical idea", a value "established by testing". Their example pair at 0.82 is two sewing-repair kits; an unrelated pair scores 0.36. With all earlier ideas kept in the model's context, only 11.7% to 13.8% of about 1,200 ideas were repeats. — [Meincke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01727)
- (2024, verified) The same authors warn that "changing the embeddings model could yield dramatically different results", that different thresholds yield different counts, and that two identical ideas written at different lengths or with redundant detail do not reach similarity 1 in practice. — [Meincke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01727)
- (current documentation) The Sentence-Transformers `community_detection` function defaults to `threshold=0.75` and `min_community_size=10`; it returns groups whose members are at least that similar to a central item. `paraphrase_mining` returns the highest-similarity pairs (defaults `top_k=100`, `max_pairs=500000`). — [Sentence-Transformers util reference](https://sbert.net/docs/package_reference/util/retrieval.html)
- (current documentation) Sentence-Transformers describes three options: k-means (cluster count must be fixed in advance, groups come out about equal in size), agglomerative clustering with a distance threshold (cluster count not needed, "only applicable for maybe a few thousand sentences"), and fast community detection ("50k sentences in less than 5 seconds"). The page gives no recommended threshold value. — [Sentence-Transformers clustering examples](https://sbert.net/examples/sentence_transformer/applications/clustering/README.html)

#### Topic-modelling tools (BERTopic and similar)
- (current documentation) BERTopic's default embedding model is all-MiniLM-L6-v2; long inputs are truncated, so "the topic model is only trained on a small part of the data", and the docs advise splitting long documents into sentences or paragraphs. Embeddings should be computed once and reused, stop words should not be removed before embedding, and the same embedding model must be used when two models are compared. The docs set `random_state` on UMAP only where they want a reproducible example. — [BERTopic tips and tricks](https://maartengr.github.io/BERTopic/getting_started/tips_and_tricks/tips_and_tricks.html)
- (current documentation) HDBSCAN leaves some documents in no topic (label -1). BERTopic offers `.reduce_outliers` with four strategies (c-TF-IDF, probabilities, topic distributions, embeddings) and a `threshold` parameter; reassigning outliers and then merging topics can cause errors. The page does not say how large the outlier share typically is. — [BERTopic outlier reduction](https://maartengr.github.io/BERTopic/getting_started/outlier_reduction/outlier_reduction.html)
- (2025 to 2026 preprint, verified) On short-text benchmarks, HDBSCAN on plain embeddings can get the cluster count badly wrong: 2 clusters estimated for the Bank77 dataset (with BERT embeddings), which has 77 true categories. — [Lin, Hasibi and Verberne, LeBoT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06747)

#### How well embeddings capture "same idea, different words" versus "same words, different idea"
- (2025, verified) On five Alternate Uses Test datasets (16,294 ideas, 1,143 participants), k-means and agglomerative clustering of embeddings produced about 588 to 838 "same idea" buckets per task where human annotators produced about 231 to 400. The best embedding-clustering agreement with human bucketing was AMI 0.39; two humans agreed with each other at AMI 0.66. The authors conclude that semantic similarity alone "over-separates true rephrasings" and that DBSCAN and HDBSCAN handle singleton and rare ideas badly (they become noise). — [Bangash et al., MuseScorer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16232)
- (2025, verified) A sentence and its simple negation stay very close in embedding space: cosine similarity roughly 0.6 to 1 across six widely used embedding models. On STS-B pairs that humans rated as near-paraphrases (0.8 to 1), the models ranked the true paraphrase above the negation only 77.25% of the time on average; for pairs rated 0.6 to 0.8, the negation was the closer one 51.57% of the time. On a second benchmark (SemAntoNeg, three choices, chance 33.33%) the newer "universal" embedding models averaged 58.64% and older contextual models 33.32%. The author attributes this to embeddings being trained mostly on topical similarity. — [Cao 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.00584)
- (2026, verified) In an evaluation of generated research questions, embedding similarity to background papers was higher for the human reference questions than for the generated ones, so embeddings did not capture the "narrowness" that human experts objected to. — [Sinhahajari, Majumder and Poria 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12071)

#### LLM-based "same idea?" checks and LLM clustering
- (2025, verified) MuseScorer retrieves up to 10 similar existing buckets by embedding, then asks an LLM (zero-shot, with a one-sentence justification) whether the new idea belongs in one of them or starts a new bucket. With llama3.3 it reached AMI 0.59 and NMI 0.88 against a human annotator (human-human AMI 0.66; best embedding clustering 0.39). Originality scores derived from its buckets correlated r of about 0.89 with human-derived scores; human-human was r = 0.77; the best clustering baseline 0.73. Stated limits: prompt sensitivity, one idea at a time, risk of hallucinated bucket assignments, tested only on Alternate Uses tasks. — [Bangash et al., MuseScorer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16232)
- (2025, secondary for the 79% figure) NoveltyBench groups model outputs into equivalence classes with a fine-tuned DeBERTa-v3-large classifier; two generations count as different "if a user would benefit from seeing both". The classifier is reported at 79% accuracy on a held-out set. — [Zhang et al., NoveltyBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05228)
- (2023, older, secondary) ClusterLLM asks an LLM pairwise or triplet questions about whether texts belong together and uses the answers to pick cluster granularity; a related approach uses the LLM as a "pairwise constraint pseudo-oracle" for constrained k-means and notes it needs many LLM queries to be effective. — [ClusterLLM, EMNLP 2023](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix-sig-urls/2023.emnlp-main.858.pdf); [NEC technical report on few-shot clustering](https://www.nec.com/en/global/techrep/journal/g23/n02/230216.html)
- (2025 to 2026 preprint, verified) Converting LLM similarity judgments into a "bag-of-texts" representation raised clustering accuracy on five short-text datasets from 46.87 to 58.43 with MiniLM embeddings and from 49.10 to 59.30 with E5-large (with the true cluster count supplied). The authors note that LLM judgments are not always transitive (A like B, B like C, but not A like C) and that each text needs LLM calls over a candidate set of 30. — [Lin, Hasibi and Verberne, LeBoT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06747)
- (2023 to 2024, verified from abstract) TopicGPT, which has an LLM write and assign natural-language topics that a user can edit, reached harmonic-mean purity 0.74 against human-annotated Wikipedia topics versus 0.64 for the strongest baseline. — [Pham et al., TopicGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01449)

### Inferences
- The two 0.8 thresholds come from different embedding models and were both tuned by eye on a handful of pairs. A team should set its own cut-off by hand-labelling a small sample of pairs (for example 50 to 100 pairs spread across the similarity range) for its own embedding model, and should expect a grey band rather than a clean line.
- A two-stage design fits a few hundred paragraphs: embeddings to shortlist the nearest neighbours of each idea, then an LLM "same idea, would a reader gain from seeing both?" check on those candidate pairs only. This is what MuseScorer does and it roughly halves the gap between embedding clustering and human agreement.
- Because LLM "same" judgments are not transitive, merging by chains of pairwise matches can glue unrelated ideas together. Assigning each idea to an existing bucket or a new one (MuseScorer's design) avoids chain merging.
- The Sentence-Transformers default `min_community_size=10` would silently drop every small group in a pool of a few hundred ideas; it has to be set to 1 or 2 for deduplication. HDBSCAN's noise label has the same effect on singletons, which are exactly the rare ideas a brainstorm wants to keep.
- Si et al. found 95% duplicates in 4,000 ideas; Meincke et al. found about 12% to 14% repeats in about 1,200. Both used a nominal 0.8 threshold and both showed the generator its earlier ideas, so the gap comes from other differences (embedding model, idea length and format, breadth of the brief, pool size). Two consequences: a duplicate rate cannot be compared between sessions unless the embedding model, threshold, idea format and pool size are held fixed, and telling the generator to avoid repeats does not prevent heavy duplication at scale.
- Paragraph-length ideas carry framing, justification and style. Similarity computed on the whole paragraph will partly measure writing style. Reducing each idea to a one- or two-sentence core claim before embedding should make similarity more about the idea; I found no study that tests this directly.

### Gaps
- No source reports a validated threshold; all are tuned by inspection. No source compares thresholds across current embedding models on idea text.
- I found no study of deduplication on product-idea paragraphs produced by many small-model agents; the closest are research ideas (Si et al.), consumer product ideas (Meincke et al.) and Alternate Uses answers (MuseScorer).
- I could not verify how Google's Co-Scientist "Proximity" step groups similar hypotheses; the full paper text did not load.
- BERTopic's documentation gives no typical outlier share and no minimum corpus size, so I cannot say how it behaves on only a few hundred paragraphs.

## 2. Diversity measures for a set of ideas

### Takeaway
The literature uses four families: average pairwise embedding similarity, counts of distinct ideas or clusters (including how fast new ideas dry up as the pool grows), "effective number" measures borrowed from ecology (Vendi Score, Hill numbers), and coverage of a predefined grid. All embedding-based measures depend on the embedding model and on text style and length, none is validated against human similarity judgments for ideas, and diversity can be raised simply by adding bad ideas, so diversity has to be reported next to quality.

### Cited Findings

#### Mean pairwise similarity
- (2024, verified) Average within-pool cosine similarity (Universal Sentence Encoder) was about 0.243 for pooled human ideas and 0.255 to 0.432 for GPT-4 across 35 prompts (base prompt 0.377; chain-of-thought prompt 0.255, the most diverse). Each prompt was run at least 10 times and results averaged over pools. — [Meincke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01727)
- (2024, verified) Authors' stated weaknesses: the measure ignores similarity to ideas that already exist in the world; "it is also not clear how it empirically links to human scored measures of similarity"; it is affected by language, style and text length; sample sizes per prompt were small so some differences may be noise; and "diversity can be obtained by sacrificing idea quality". — [Meincke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01727)
- (2024, verified) Doshi and Hauser measured story similarity as cosine similarity of OpenAI embeddings. Access to GPT-4 ideas raised similarity among stories by 10.7% (one idea) and 8.9% (five ideas) of the human-only similarity range, while rated novelty rose 5.4% to 8.1% and usefulness 3.7% to 9.0%. N = 293 writers, 600 evaluators, 3,519 evaluations. — [Doshi and Hauser 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532)
- (2025 survey, secondary) A survey of LLM scientific idea generation notes that one dense cluster of similar ideas pulls mean pairwise similarity up and so lowers the diversity score for the whole set. — [Creativity-centred survey, arXiv 2511.07448](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.07448)
- (2021, older, secondary) Work on crowd ideation lists alternatives to the mean: minimum pairwise distance (sensitive to redundancy, blind to far-apart points), Chamfer distance (distance to nearest neighbour) and span (spread around a centroid). — [Cox et al., Directed Diversity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.06030)

#### Number of unique ideas and exhaustion as the pool grows
- (2024, verified) Meincke et al. count an idea as unique if its similarity to every earlier idea is below 0.8, then apply a mark-and-recapture style estimate (from Kornish and Ulrich) to the total and unique counts. Estimated reachable idea space: about 3,700 for the base prompt and about 4,700 for chain-of-thought. They stress this estimates only what one technique can reach, not the true space. "Speed of exhaustion" is the smoothed maximum similarity of each new idea to all earlier ones. — [Meincke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01727)
- (2011, older, abstract only) Kornish and Ulrich analysed 1,368 opportunities in five data sets, found redundancy "quite small in absolute terms" among human ideas, and estimated about 1,000 unique ideas for the narrowest domain and more than 2,000 for broader ones. — [Kornish and Ulrich 2011](https://repository.upenn.edu/fnce_papers/104)
- (2024, verified) In Si et al., the cumulative number of non-duplicate ideas plateaued at about 200 of 4,000 generated. — [Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109)
- (2025, secondary) NoveltyBench's `distinct_k` is the number of equivalence classes among k samples; `utility_k` credits only generations that fall in a new class and weights them by a quality score. Across 20 models, outputs were less diverse than human writers, and larger models in a family were often less diverse than smaller ones. — [Zhang et al., NoveltyBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05228)
- (2025, press summaries only) Meincke, Nave and Terwiesch's re-analysis in Nature Human Behaviour reported lower idea diversity with ChatGPT in 37 of 45 comparisons; press coverage quotes 94% of ChatGPT-assisted ideas sharing overlapping concepts in one toy task. — [Mack Institute announcement](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/2025/new-in-nature-chatgpt-decreases-idea-diversity-in-brainstorming/); [Penn Today](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/wharton-does-ai-limit-creativity)

#### Measures borrowed from ecology
- (2022 to 2023, older, verified from abstract) The Vendi Score is "the exponential of the Shannon entropy of the eigenvalues of a similarity matrix" built from a user-chosen similarity function. It needs no reference set and no predefined categories. — [Friedman and Dieng](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02410)
- (2023 to 2024, verified from abstract) The Vendi Score extends the Hill number of order q = 1. It weights items in proportion to their prevalence, which the authors call undesirable when prevalence is very unbalanced; a family of scores with other orders q lets the user put more weight on rare or on common items. — [Pasarkar and Dieng](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12952)
- (2025, verified) Frequency-based originality: once ideas are bucketed, an idea's originality is a function of how rare its bucket is. MuseScorer tested four such metrics (rarity, Shapley, uniqueness, threshold); the tier cut-offs in the preferred "threshold" metric are an unvalidated heuristic. — [Bangash et al., MuseScorer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16232)

#### Coverage of a predefined grid
- (2023, older, abstract only) Quality-Diversity through AI Feedback has a language model rate both the quality and the diversity attributes of each text and reports that it "covers more of a specified search space with high-quality samples" than controls; human evaluation showed "reasonable agreement" with the AI ratings (no number in the abstract). — [Bradley et al., QDAIF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13032)

#### Multi-agent sessions specifically
- (2026, abstract only) Chen et al. report "diversity collapse" in multi-agent idea generation: stronger models show diminishing marginal diversity, adding agents gives diminishing returns, authority-driven dynamics and dense communication speed up convergence. The abstract gives no numbers and does not name the metrics. — [Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18005)
- (2026, abstract only) Multi-agent debate "actively suppresses output diversity across independent runs"; the proposed fix gives each agent a persistent distinct "cognitive lens" and shows it only its most semantically distant peers. No numbers in the abstract. — [Nguyen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00683)
- (2026, abstract only) A 2026 tool paper states that embedding-based diversity measurement is "inconsistent and fragmented" with no standard tooling. — [Su et al., emb-diversity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.19848)

### Inferences
- To compare a 10-agent and a 33-agent session, counts of clusters or unique ideas must be compared at equal pool size (for example by repeatedly sampling the same number of ideas from each session), because a larger pool will always contain more distinct ideas. Mean pairwise similarity is less sensitive to pool size but is distorted by one large cluster. This is my reasoning from how the measures are defined; no source I read tests it on brainstorm data.
- A practical scorecard that follows the literature: (a) share of ideas that are unique after deduplication, (b) the curve of unique ideas against ideas generated, (c) an effective-number measure such as Vendi on the same embedding model for both sessions, (d) coverage of a grid the team defines in advance (for example user segment by problem type), and (e) a quality measure for the best few ideas. No single number is trusted in the sources.
- Because Meincke et al. needed at least 10 runs per prompt and still flagged noise, one session per design is not enough to claim that a new design is better. Repeated sessions, or resampling within sessions, are needed to see whether the difference exceeds run-to-run variation.
- Coverage of a predefined grid is the only measure here that does not depend on embeddings, but it depends on an LLM or person assigning each idea to a cell, and it cannot reward an idea that falls outside the grid.

### Gaps
- No source validates any embedding-based diversity measure against human judgments of idea-set diversity. A search snippet claimed the Vendi Score reached 87% alignment with human judgments in one idea-diversity check; I could not trace it to a paper and have not used it.
- I found no source applying rarefaction or species-richness estimators other than the Kornish and Ulrich recapture estimate to idea pools.
- Numbers for the 2026 multi-agent diversity papers and the Nature Human Behaviour paper were not retrieved from primary text.
- The exact similarity method in Kornish and Ulrich (who rated similarity, on what scale) was not retrieved.

## 3. Quality and novelty judging by LLMs versus human experts

### Takeaway
Across studies from 2024 to 2026, LLM judges agree with expert ratings of idea novelty and quality only weakly (correlations at or below about 0.35, agreement near or below chance in some settings), and experts themselves agree only moderately (56% to 61% pairwise). The best-documented LLM-judge biases are position, self-preference, sensitivity to tone and to stated majority or authority, score clustering in an upper-middle band, and a preference for ambitious, novel-sounding framing; the "favours safe ideas" bias is documented mainly for human experts, not for LLM judges.

### Cited Findings

#### Agreement with human experts on ideas
- (2024, verified) Si et al.: balanced-accuracy agreement of each evaluator with the others was 56.1% for human reviewers of ideas (versus 66.0% at NeurIPS 2021 and 71.9% at ICLR 2024 for full papers; 50% is chance). LLM evaluators: Claude-3.5 pairwise 53.3%, Claude-3.5 direct 51.7%, GPT-4o direct 50.0%, GPT-4o pairwise 45.0%, the "AI Scientist" reviewer 43.3%. The authors conclude LLMs "cannot evaluate ideas reliably yet" and warn that AI reviewers may trade variance for bias. — [Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109)
- (2026, verified) Bao et al. collected 25,139 ratings from 6,749 scientists on hypotheses that 26 LLMs generated for the scientists' own papers. No LLM judge exceeded r = 0.35 with human ratings on any dimension (best: a retrieval-augmented deep-research judge). On a held-out pairwise novelty test LLM judges were near chance. A fine-tuned reward model reached about 64% to 69% pairwise accuracy on novelty, against a human-reviewer consistency benchmark of 61.0% pairwise agreement on 26,731 OpenReview submissions. — [Bao et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08251)
- (2026, verified) Sinhahajari et al.: on 50 blinded pairs, two domain experts preferred the human-anchored research question over the best-of-five model question on non-obviousness (78% versus 14%, and 56% versus 38%); the LLM judges preferred the model question 82% (gemini-3.1-pro) and 52% (deepseek-v4-pro) of the time. Expert-expert agreement was 60%; expert-LLM agreement 22% to 40%. The authors call this a "novelty mirage". A prompt revised to penalise narrowness did not close the gap. — [Sinhahajari et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12071)
- (2026, verified) HindSight scored 200 generated research ideas by whether similar papers later appeared and how cited they were. An LLM judge saw no difference between two generators (7.44 versus 7.40, p = 0.584) where the outcome measure saw a 2.5-fold gap. For one generator, LLM-judged novelty correlated negatively with the outcome measure (rho = -0.29, p = 0.003) and LLM-judged feasibility positively (rho = 0.25). Limits: one judge model, 200 ideas, and the outcome measure itself may undervalue ideas unlike existing work. — [Jiang 2026, HindSight](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15164)
- (2026, verified from abstract) For 300 patent-grounded product ideas with about 3,000 expert scores on six dimensions, experts disagreed substantially on fine-grained ordinal scores but agreed more under coarse selection. LLM judges conditioned on one evaluator's past scores matched that evaluator better than a judge conditioned on pooled scores; the authors call pooled labels "a fragile target". No agreement numbers in the abstract. — [Hirota et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22517)
- (2025, verified from abstract) In 50 LLM-generated research documents, 13 experts judged 24% to be paraphrased or heavily borrowed from existing work; automated plagiarism detectors were "inadequate" at catching this. — [Gupta and Pruthi 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.16487)

#### Agreement on short creativity-test answers (a much easier task)
- (2023, older, secondary via the authors' site) Fine-tuned LLM scoring of divergent-thinking answers reached up to r = .81 with human raters. — [Ocsai](https://openscoring.du.edu/ocsai), reporting [Organisciak et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tsc.2023.101356)
- (2024, secondary) For metaphor creativity, fine-tuned RoBERTa (r = .72) and GPT-2 (r = .70) predicted human ratings better than semantic distance (r = .42). — [DiStefano et al.](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10525807-automatic-scoring-metaphor-creativity-large-language-models)
- (2024 to 2025, secondary) LLM judges of Alternate Uses answers agreed with each other at Spearman above 0.7 on average and did not favour their own model's answers. — [Do LLMs Agree on the Creativity Evaluation of Alternative Uses?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15560)
- (2025, secondary) Human judges agreed unanimously on only about 58% of Alternate Uses answers in one validation study. — [Kucwaj and Kroczek 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-3200/13/6/64)
- (2009, older, secondary) In the Consensual Assessment Technique, inter-rater reliability among expert judges is generally .80 to .90 and rises with the number of judges; experts' and novices' mean ratings correlated only .71 in one study, and novices were less consistent. — [Kaufman, Baer and Cole](https://www.rider.edu/sites/default/files/2024-05/ExpertiseDomains%26CAT.pdf)

#### Documented biases, with numbers
- Position (2023, older, verified): with answer order swapped, GPT-4 gave consistent verdicts 65.0% of the time, GPT-3.5 46.2%, Claude-v1 23.8% (Claude-v1 favoured the first answer 75% of the time). — [Zheng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)
- Position (2024 to 2025, verified): robustness rate (share of verdicts unchanged after the perturbation) 0.566 for GPT-3.5, 0.776 for GPT-4o, 0.818 for GPT-4-Turbo, 0.832 for Claude-3.5. Position bias grows when three or four answers are compared at once; most models fell below 0.5. — [Ye et al., CALM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02736)
- Self-preference (2024 to 2025, verified): relative score gap between a judge's own answers and others' ranged from 1.16% (GPT-4-Turbo) to 7.48% (Claude-3.5) and 16.1% (Qwen2). — [Ye et al., CALM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02736)
- Self-preference (2023 to 2024, older): GPT-4 rated itself about 10% and Claude-v1 about 25% above human win rates, though the authors call the data too limited to conclude. A GPT-4 judge ranked another GPT-4 variant second when humans ranked it fourth, and each judge's largest positive deviation came when judging its own output. GPT-4 and Llama 2 can recognise their own text, and self-recognition correlates linearly with self-preference after fine-tuning. — [Zheng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685); [Verga et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796); [Panickssery et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076)
- Verbosity (2023, older, verified): a padded "repetitive list" answer fooled GPT-3.5 and Claude-v1 91.3% of the time and GPT-4 8.7%. In 2024, robustness to lengthening without improvement was 0.884 to 0.977 across six judges. — [Zheng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685); [Ye et al., CALM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02736)
- Tone, majority and authority (2024 to 2025, verified): rewriting the better answer in a fearful tone dropped robustness to 0.24 to 0.36 for five of six judges; stating that a majority prefers one answer gave 0.56 to 0.80; adding a fake book citation or quote gave 0.63 to 0.86. — [Ye et al., CALM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02736)
- Score clustering (2024, secondary): on a 1 to 100 scale judges favour round numbers such as 90 and 95, and a high score on one criterion pulls up the next criterion's score. — [Stureborg et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01724)
- Score clustering (2026, secondary): aligned (post-safety-training) models cluster scores, often at high values, and stronger clustering goes with lower evaluation accuracy; the most effective fix tested was changing the allowed score range in the prompt. — [Exploring the Effects of Alignment on Numerical Bias](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16444)
- Score clustering on ideas (2026, verified): LLM ratings of hypotheses clustered in the upper-middle of the scale; explicitly encouraging extreme scores raised the standard deviation by only 0.33 on a 1 to 9 scale. Standalone novelty scoring of research questions produced ties in 44% to 59% of comparisons. — [Bao et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08251); [Sinhahajari et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12071)
- Framing (2026, verified): LLM judges favoured ambitious, broadly framed ideas ("holistic frameworks", "unified approaches") that matched no later paper, and underrated technically specific ideas; the author warns that optimising generators against LLM-judge scores may favour "impressive-sounding but ultimately vacuous ideas". — [Jiang 2026, HindSight](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15164)
- Safe-idea bias in humans (2026, verified): scientists rated hypotheses resembling their own work higher on feasibility (coefficient 2.29) and probability (3.42) and lower on novelty (-3.15); adoption was driven mostly by perceived probability (0.31) rather than novelty (0.12). — [Bao et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08251)

#### Counter-measures reported
- (2023, older, verified) Judge each pair in both orders and count inconsistent verdicts as a tie. Few-shot examples raised GPT-4's order consistency from 65.0% to 77.5% at about four times the cost. Chain-of-thought and reference answers cut failure rates on maths grading from 70% to 30% and 15%. — [Zheng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)
- (2024 to 2025, verified) Use a different model to judge than the one that generated; randomise order; hide model identity; add protective instructions; use step-by-step reasoning; note that biases matter most when the quality gap between items is small. — [Ye et al., CALM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02736)
- (2024, verified) Si et al. passed every idea, human or AI, through an LLM "style normalization" step that converts all ideas "into the same writing and formatting style without changing the original content" before review; in a small pilot, expert judges could then tell AI from human ideas only about 50% of the time. — [Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109)
- (2024, verified) A panel of three small models from different families (Command R, Claude Haiku, GPT-3.5), aggregated by vote or average, correlated better with human rankings than a single GPT-4 judge (Pearson 0.917 versus 0.817; Kendall tau 0.778 versus 0.667), with a smaller spread of deviations from human scores, at one-seventh to one-eighth of the cost. A single GPT-4 judge's agreement with humans swung from kappa 0.518 to 0.725 with prompt wording alone. Tested on question answering and chatbot rankings, not ideas. — [Verga et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796)
- (2026, secondary) Averaging rubric scores over balanced permutations of presentation order exposes hidden position bias and improves correlation with human judgments. — [Xu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02219)
- (2025, secondary) Using the judge's full probability distribution over scores, rather than its single most likely score, outperformed the mode in pointwise, pairwise and listwise settings. — [Wang, Zhang and Choi 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.03064)
- (2026, verified) Use more than one judge and check their agreement; score narrowness or scope as its own dimension; do not treat LLM novelty scores as reliable alone. — [Sinhahajari et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12071)

### Inferences
- The evidence splits by task difficulty. Where the judgment is short and well-defined (creativity-test answers, factual question answering, chatbot answers), LLM judges reach human-level agreement. Where it is "is this idea new and good" (research ideas, hypotheses, research questions), they do not, and the 2026 studies with current-generation judges show the gap has not closed.
- For the team's case, an LLM judge is defensible as a coarse filter (removing clearly weak or off-brief ideas, and sorting within a theme) but not as the final arbiter of novelty. Novelty also needs a prior-art check that the judge cannot do from the idea text alone.
- If the same small model that generated the ideas also judges them, self-preference is not the main risk (all ideas come from the same model); the larger risk is shared blind spots, which Bao et al. describe as judges and judged ideas sharing the same priors. A judge from a different model family addresses both.
- The tone, authority and majority results, together with Si et al.'s style-normalization step, imply that idea paragraphs should be rewritten into one common format before judging: similar length, neutral tone, no claims about popularity or who proposed it, no agent names.
- Since fine scores cluster and experts agree more on coarse selection, a three-way decision (keep, maybe, drop) or a top-k pick per theme is better supported than a 1 to 10 score.

### Gaps
- No study I found measures LLM judges on product ideas for a consumer app against the judgments of a small product team. The closest is Hirota et al. (patent-grounded product ideas), for which I have only the abstract.
- Evidence that LLM judges themselves favour safe ideas is thin; the documented direction is the opposite (novel-sounding framing). A search snippet mentioned a study finding a "conciseness preference" rather than verbosity bias in newer judges; I did not verify it.
- The semantic-distance baseline figures in Organisciak et al. 2023 were not retrieved from the paper.
- A search snippet reported expert agreement on feasibility of ICC = 0.453 ("poor") in one study of generated research ideas, with better agreement on originality and clarity; I could not pin down the source paper.
- Stureborg et al. and the numerical-bias paper were read only through summaries.

## 4. Ranking methods: absolute scores, pairwise comparison, tournaments and panels

### Takeaway
Pairwise comparison generally tracks human judgment better than absolute scoring when items differ in quality, but it forces a winner between near-equal items, amplifies distractor features and can amplify a judge's bias toward novel-sounding text; absolute scoring is steadier but clusters. For a few hundred items, the comparative-judgement literature and LLM tournament papers point to each item appearing in roughly 5 to 20 comparisons (about 750 to 3,000 comparisons in total for 300 items), fitted with an order-independent model, rather than a full round robin of about 45,000.

### Cited Findings

#### Absolute versus pairwise
- (2024, verified) Si et al. found LLMs "poorly calibrated" when asked to predict scores or accept/reject decisions directly, and better at pairwise choice: on 1,200 ICLR 2024 submissions, Claude-3.5-Sonnet picked the accepted paper of a pair 71.4% of the time (GPT-4o 61.1%, Claude-3-Opus 63.5%). Few-shot and chain-of-thought prompts gave no significant gain. — [Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109)
- (2023 to 2024, verified from abstract) For moderate-sized open models (FlanT5, Llama2-chat), pairwise comparative assessment is "superior to prompt scoring"; LLMs show strong position bias in pairwise comparisons and debiasing improves results. — [Liusie, Manakul and Gales](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.07889)
- (2024 to 2025, verified) PairS: Spearman correlation with human judgments for GPT-4-turbo rose from 0.55, 0.44 and 0.35 (direct scoring on three datasets) to 0.64, 0.53 and 0.44 with pairwise merge-sort ranking; for GPT-3.5 from 0.44, 0.32 and 0.22 to 0.56, 0.42 and 0.38. Recalibrating score distributions did not fix direct scoring. Pairwise gave little benefit to small models on long creative-writing texts and did poorly where most items share the same human score. — [Liu et al., PairS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.16950)
- (2023, older, verified) On chatbot answers, GPT-4 pairwise and single-answer grading agreed with humans equally (85% and 84% to 85%; human-human 81% to 82%). — [Zheng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)
- (2025, verified) Tripathi et al.: when a distractor feature such as assertive tone is added, pairwise preferences flip in about 35% of cases versus 9% for absolute scores. For pairs of equal quality, absolute scoring returned a tie 84.6% to 93.2% of the time; pairwise returned a tie 2.4% to 7.3% of the time. Under pairwise Elo, weak models rose noticeably after assertiveness edits. Pairwise preferences were intransitive 7.6% to 10.8% of the time and changed with the choice set up to 17.0%. Judges were open models. — [Tripathi et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14716)
- (2026, verified) Switching from standalone to comparative novelty judging raised the LLM judges' win rate for model-generated questions from 27.2% to 49.1% and cut ties from 59.1% to 36.8%, moving further from the experts, who preferred the human questions. — [Sinhahajari et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12071)

#### Tournaments, Elo and Swiss systems
- (2024, verified) Si et al. ranked ideas with a Swiss-system tournament of LLM pairwise comparisons, pairing ideas with similar running scores. Five rounds was best on validation: the gap in true review scores between the top-10 and bottom-10 ranked papers grew from 0.56 after one round to 1.73 after five, and fell to 1.30 after six. Yet of the 49 ideas a human expert picked from the same pool, only 17 overlapped with the AI ranker's top 49, and the human-picked set scored higher on every metric (overall 5.34 versus 4.85). — [Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109)
- (2023, older, secondary) Elo ratings of LLM outputs depend on the order in which comparisons are processed and on the K-factor; transitivity does not reliably hold between items of similar quality; ratings stabilise when averaged over 100 or more random orderings. — [Boubdir et al., Elo Uncovered](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17295)
- (2025 to 2026, secondary) Google's Co-Scientist ranks hypotheses with an Elo tournament of pairwise comparisons, using multi-turn debates for the comparisons; its authors report that higher Elo tracks higher accuracy on a question-answering benchmark and that the debate format nearly removed order dependence. This is the authors' own claim; I found no independent replication. — [Gottweis et al., Co-Scientist](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18864)

#### Panels of judges or models
- (2024, verified) A three-model panel from different families beat a single large judge on agreement with humans and cost (figures in section 3). — [Verga et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796)
- (2026, verified from abstract) Where experts hold different standards, a pooled or averaged judge is "a fragile target"; judges modelled on individual evaluators fit better. — [Hirota et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22517)

#### How many comparisons
- (2019, older, secondary) Verhavert et al., meta-analysis of 49 human comparative-judgement assessments: each item needs to appear in 10 to 14 comparisons for reliability .70, and in 26 to 37 for .90. — [Verhavert et al. 2019](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-on-the-reliability-of-comparative-judgement/)
- (2025, verified) Kinnear, Jones and Davies, 101 human comparative-judgement sessions (median about 40 items and 22 judges): the number of comparisons per item was the only significant predictor of reliability. They recommend about 10 comparisons per item counted as total comparisons divided by items (so each item appears in about 20), and a reliability target (scale separation reliability) of .8 rather than .7. Adaptive pairing inflates the reliability statistic (mean .92 versus .85 for non-adaptive sessions at similar comparison counts). About 9% of sessions with the statistic at .8 or above still had split-half reliability below .7. — [Kinnear et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12246014)
- (2021, older, secondary) A simulation study argues the reliability estimate is unstable at low counts and recommends 41 comparisons per item, noting this depends on the number of items and true variance. This conflicts with the two meta-analyses. — [Frontiers in Education 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2021.788202)
- (2024 to 2025, verified) Merge-sort ranking with an LLM needs on the order of N log N comparisons; on one benchmark it matched Elo with about 30% of the comparisons. A scaling variant ranks 100 anchor items fully and places the rest by binary search. — [Liu et al., PairS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.16950)

### Inferences
- Arithmetic for 300 items: a full round robin is 44,850 comparisons. A five-round Swiss tournament is 750 comparisons (each idea compared five times). Kinnear's recommendation is about 3,000 comparisons (each idea in about 20). Verhavert's figures imply about 1,500 to 2,100 comparisons for reliability .70 and about 3,900 to 5,550 for .90. Merge sort is at most about 2,500. Judging each pair in both orders to control position bias doubles these.
- The comparative-judgement figures come from human judges, who are noisy but not systematically biased in the same direction. With one LLM judge, more comparisons shrink noise but not bias; Si et al.'s five-round optimum and their mismatch with the human pick both suggest that beyond a few rounds the limit is the judge, not the comparison count.
- Si et al.'s tournament separated top from bottom well but disagreed with the expert on which ideas were best. That supports using a tournament to discard the bottom half or two thirds, not to choose the final shortlist.
- Because Elo depends on processing order, fitting a Bradley-Terry model to all comparisons at once (or averaging Elo over many shuffles) is the safer way to turn comparisons into a ranking.
- Pairwise forcing is harmful exactly where a brainstorm pool is densest: many near-equal ideas. Allowing an explicit "no real difference" answer, and ranking within themes after deduplication rather than across the whole pool, follows from Tripathi et al. and PairS.
- CALM's finding that position bias worsens with three or four options argues against asking a judge to rank a long list in one prompt.

### Gaps
- I found no study that directly measures how many LLM pairwise comparisons give a stable ranking of a few hundred ideas, nor one comparing Elo, Bradley-Terry and Swiss on idea pools. The Liusie et al. result on using a subset of comparisons was not retrieved beyond the abstract.
- Co-Scientist's tournament details and accuracy figures were not verified in the paper text.
- No source tests panels of judges on idea novelty or usefulness specifically; panel evidence is from question answering and chatbot ranking.
- The Verhavert figures come from a search summary of the abstract.

## 5. Where human judgement is still required, and how to spend little of it

### Takeaway
Authors of the idea-evaluation studies say humans are still needed for the final novelty and quality call, for prior-art checks, and for anything that depends on execution; LLM judges are positioned as fast first-pass screens. The lowest-cost patterns with evidence behind them are coarse rather than fine human decisions, eliminating the worst ideas rather than rating all of them, and validating the LLM judge on a small human-labelled sample before trusting it.

### Cited Findings
- (2024, verified) "LLMs cannot evaluate ideas reliably yet"; a single human expert manually choosing from the same generated pool produced a better-rated set than the LLM ranker (overall 5.34 versus 4.85 on a 10-point scale; novelty 5.81 versus 5.64). Reviewing ideas is "inherently subjective": human reviewers agreed with each other only 56.1%. — [Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109)
- (2025, verified) When 43 researchers actually executed ideas, scores of LLM-generated ideas fell far more than those of human ideas (overall -1.98 versus -0.63; effectiveness -1.88 versus -0.05; novelty -1.05 versus -0.01), erasing the LLM ideas' advantage at the idea stage. Reviews written before execution "often speculate" on the assumption that the method will work; execution exposed problems the idea-stage review missed. The authors recommend taking execution outcomes into account. — [Si, Hashimoto and Yang 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.20803)
- (2026, verified) "Automated evaluators should not be treated as validated, since judges and judged ideas share the same priors"; claims should rest on whether domain experts adopt the ideas. — [Bao et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08251)
- (2026, verified) LLM-as-judge is "useful for fast screening"; where an outcome-based check exists, report both. — [Jiang 2026, HindSight](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15164)
- (2025, verified from abstract) Expert review, cross-checked with original authors, was needed to find that 24% of LLM-generated research documents were paraphrased or heavily borrowed; automated detectors missed them. — [Gupta and Pruthi 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.16487)
- (2023 working paper, older, secondary) Girotra, Meincke, Terwiesch and Ulrich: of the 40 best ideas (top 10%) in a pooled set judged by purchase-intent surveys, 35 came from ChatGPT and 5 from students; average purchase probability was 40% for human ideas, 47% for GPT-4 and 49% for GPT-4 seeded with good examples. Terwiesch has said this moves the bottleneck from generating ideas to evaluating them. — [Girotra et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4526071); [Penn summary](https://improvinghealthcare.mehp.upenn.edu/thought-leadership/generative-ai-innovation-engine)
- (2015, older, abstract only) "Bag of lemons": giving evaluators a small budget of tokens to mark the worst ideas filtered an idea pool more accurately than Likert-scale rating in "only a fraction of the time", because "crowds are much better at eliminating bad ideas than at identifying good ones". The abstract gives no numbers. — [Klein and Garcia 2015](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/114252)
- (2018, older, abstract only) DBLemons combines worst-idea voting with a diversity-based ranking that shows voters a wider spread of the idea space; it "reduces the idea space in half while still retaining 94% of the top ideas" and was more accurate and faster than majority voting. — [Lykourentzou et al. 2018](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/when-crowds-give-you-lemons-filtering-innovative-ideas-using-a-di/)
- (2023, abstract only) The accuracy of lemon-style filtering depends on the size of the token budget (experiments with 850 subjects). — [Lukumon and Klein 2023](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/decisn/v50y2023i2d10.1007_s40622-023-00349-w.html)
- (2026, verified from abstract) Experts agree more on coarse selection than on fine ordinal scores. — [Hirota et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22517)
- (2025, abstract plus secondary) The Alternative Annotator Test is a statistical procedure for deciding whether an LLM can stand in for human annotators using "only a modest subset of annotated examples"; a search summary of the paper gives at least three human annotators and 50 to 100 examples. Across ten datasets, LLMs could "sometimes" replace humans, and prompting technique changed judge quality. — [Calderon, Reichart and Dror 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.10970)
- (2009, older, secondary) Reliability of human creativity ratings rises with the number of judges; expert panels typically reach .80 to .90, and novices agree less with each other and with experts. — [Kaufman, Baer and Cole](https://www.rider.edu/sites/default/files/2024-05/ExpertiseDomains%26CAT.pdf)

### Inferences
- A workable division of labour that follows these sources: the machine deduplicates, groups, and removes the clearly weak; people make coarse keep-or-drop calls on one representative per group and on the singletons; the machine's ranking inside a group is advisory.
- With about a dozen themes and perhaps a few dozen singleton ideas after deduplication, two or three people marking "lemons" on representatives is a short session, and the lemon results suggest it is also the more accurate use of their time than scoring everything.
- Before trusting an LLM judge on a new session, have two or three people label 50 to 100 ideas (keep, maybe, drop) and check the judge against them. If the judge agrees with the people about as well as they agree with each other, use it for the bulk; if not, restrict it to deduplication and grouping.
- More than one person should judge. A single coordinator reading and grouping everything, as in the team's two sessions, has no agreement check, and human-human agreement on ideas in the literature is only 56% to 61%.
- The ideation-execution result means the shortlist is a set of bets, not a ranking of value. A cheap execution probe (a mock-up, a one-day spike, five user conversations) is the only evaluation step the literature shows changing the verdict.

### Gaps
- I found no study in which humans rated only cluster representatives and the result was compared with rating every idea. DBLemons is the closest (diversity-aware exposure plus elimination), and I have only its abstract.
- Klein and Garcia's time and accuracy figures were not retrieved.
- The 50 to 100 examples and three annotators for the Alternative Annotator Test come from a search summary, not the abstract.
- No source quantifies how much human time is saved by any of these schemes on LLM-generated idea pools.

## 6. The novelty-versus-feasibility trade-off and keeping wild ideas alive

### Takeaway
At the idea stage, LLM-generated research ideas are rated more novel and slightly (not significantly) less feasible than expert ideas, but the novelty advantage disappears once ideas are executed, and in a business-idea contest the pattern was reversed (human ideas more novel, AI-assisted ideas more valuable). Human evaluators reliably discount novel ideas in favour of feasible ones, so a selection scheme that averages scores will rank wild ideas out unless novelty and feasibility are judged separately and some places are reserved by design.

### Cited Findings

#### What studies of AI-generated ideas report
- (2024, verified) Si et al., 79 expert reviewers, 49 ideas per condition, 298 reviews, 10-point scales. Novelty: human 4.84, AI 5.64, AI plus human rerank 5.81 (both significant). Feasibility: human 6.61, AI 6.34, rerank 6.44 (not significant; the authors say the study lacked power to confirm it). Overall score correlated with excitement (r = 0.854) and novelty (r = 0.725) but hardly with feasibility (r = 0.097). — [Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109)
- (2025, verified) After execution by 43 researchers and review by 58, AI ideas no longer led on any measure: novelty 4.73 versus 4.90 for human ideas, excitement 3.90 versus 4.48, effectiveness 4.13 versus 4.78, overall 3.41 versus 3.97 (per-idea differences not significant; per-review differences significant except novelty). The drop from idea stage to execution was significantly larger for AI ideas on all four measures. — [Si, Hashimoto and Yang 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.20803)
- (2024, abstract only) Boussioux et al., circular-economy business ideas, 300 evaluators, 234 solutions, 3,900 evaluator-solution pairs: human-crowd solutions were more novel, on average and at the top end; human-guided AI solutions scored higher on strategic viability, financial and environmental value and overall quality. Prompting the model to differ from its earlier outputs ("differentiated search") beat independent runs. — [Boussioux et al. 2024](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ororsc:v:35:y:2024:i:5:p:1589-1607)
- (2023, older, secondary) Girotra et al. argue that the inconsistency of LLMs raises the variance of idea quality and that this improves the quality of the best ideas, which is what matters in innovation. — [Girotra et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4526071)
- (2026, verified) For one generator, ideas an LLM judge rated most novel did worst on a later-outcome measure (rho = -0.29), while judged feasibility related positively (rho = 0.25). — [Jiang 2026, HindSight](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15164)
- (2011, older, abstract only) Among human-generated opportunities, ideas with more similar neighbours tended to be more valuable: "the ideas that are least similar to others are not generally the most valuable ones". — [Kornish and Ulrich 2011](https://repository.upenn.edu/fnce_papers/104)
- (2024, verified) Individual gain, collective loss: GPT-4 idea prompts made stories more novel and useful (up to +8.1% and +9.0%) but more similar to each other. — [Doshi and Hauser 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532)

#### How evaluators treat novel ideas
- (2010, older, abstract via aggregator) Rietzschel, Nijstad and Stroebe: people selecting from their own brainstormed ideas showed a "strong tendency to select feasible and desirable ideas, at the cost of originality". Instructing them to select creative ideas raised the originality of the selection but lowered their satisfaction and the rated effectiveness of the chosen ideas. — [Rietzschel et al. 2010](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/912990346)
- (2014, older, abstract) Only under explicit creativity instructions did participants select more creative ideas. — [Rietzschel et al. 2014](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/effects-of-problem-scope-and-creativity-instructions-on-idea-gene/)
- (2023 working paper, secondary) In a NASA robotics challenge with 374 evaluators and 101 solutions, domain experts weighed feasibility roughly 30% more than other evaluators when rating quality, discounting riskier, more novel designs; the effect was weaker for evaluators with expertise spanning two domains. — [HBS Working Knowledge on Lane et al.](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/when-experts-play-it-too-safe-innovation-lessons-from-a-nasa-experiment)
- (2016, older, secondary) In medical grant review, a one-standard-deviation increase in a proposal's novelty lowered its expected rank by 4.5 percentile points, and only a minority of that penalty was explained by lower perceived feasibility. — [Boudreau et al.](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/1/10001229/1/13-053.pdf)
- (2026, verified) Scientists penalised the novelty of hypotheses unlike their own work and adopted on perceived probability of being true. — [Bao et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08251)

#### Selection schemes that protect unusual ideas
- (2023, older, abstract only) Quality-diversity search keeps the best candidate for each region of a predefined attribute space instead of one global ranking, with an LLM assessing both quality and attributes. — [Bradley et al., QDAIF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13032)
- (2018, older, abstract only) Diversity-aware filtering that balances idea quality against coverage of the idea space kept 94% of top ideas while halving the pool. — [Lykourentzou et al. 2018](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/when-crowds-give-you-lemons-filtering-innovative-ideas-using-a-di/)
- (2025 to 2026, abstract) Co-Scientist pairs its ranking tournament with an evolution step that keeps refining hypotheses rather than only discarding them. — [Gottweis et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18864)
- (2026, verified from abstract) Disagreement among expert evaluators of business ideas reflects different standards rather than noise, so pooling scores can erase it. — [Hirota et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22517)

### Inferences
- Novelty and feasibility should be scored as separate dimensions and never averaged into one number. An averaged score reproduces the human bias toward feasible ideas, and in Si et al. the "overall" score barely reflected feasibility at all, so a single score is unstable in either direction.
- A portfolio rule follows from the quality-diversity and diversity-aware filtering work: keep the best idea in each theme or grid cell, then add a small reserved quota of high-novelty ideas regardless of feasibility score. Ideas on which judges disagree strongly are candidates for the quota rather than for rejection; this last point is my extension of Hirota et al., and I found no study that tests it.
- Rietzschel's result suggests the instruction given to human selectors matters: telling people explicitly to pick some ideas for originality changes what they pick, at the cost of them feeling less sure about the picks.
- Idea-stage novelty ratings of LLM ideas are inflated relative to what survives execution. Wild ideas kept in the shortlist should therefore go to a cheap test quickly, not be treated as proven by their novelty score.
- The Kornish and Ulrich result is a caution against treating outliers as valuable by default: in human idea pools, the most isolated ideas were not generally the best. Keeping some wild ideas is a hedge, not an expectation that they will win.

### Gaps
- I found no study that tests a reserved quota, a disagreement-based rule, or any other "keep wild ideas" scheme against plain ranking on LLM-generated ideas.
- Boussioux et al.'s mean scores and Girotra et al.'s novelty comparison were not retrieved from the papers; a secondary site gave means for Boussioux that I did not use.
- The Rietzschel, Lane and Boudreau findings were read as abstracts or secondary summaries.
- All large human-versus-AI comparisons are in research, business-plan or consumer-product domains; none concerns feature ideas for an existing product, where feasibility depends on the team's own codebase and resources.

# Trust, safety and abuse risks in AI-agent commerce and negotiation (as of 17 November 2026)

Method note: these notes rest on about 25 web searches run on 17 Nov 2026. Most findings come from search-result summaries of the linked pages; I did not open the primary documents (papers, RBI report, Parliament replies, protocol specs) in full. Where a number comes from press coverage of a primary source, that is stated. Confidence labels: High = several independent sources agree or the source is primary; Medium = one credible source or secondary coverage of a primary; Low = single vendor/blog source or conflicting figures.

## 1. Prompt injection and manipulation in customer-facing and negotiating agents

### Takeaway
Every well-known public incident was a user talking a bot into saying something the business never authorised, and the one tribunal that ruled on it held the business responsible for the bot's statement. Research in 2025-2026 shows model-level and filter defences fail against determined attackers (over 95% attack success against defences that looked near-perfect on static tests), so the only safeguards with evidence behind them sit outside the model: hard limits in code, restricted action sets, and human confirmation for irreversible steps.

### Cited Findings

Documented incidents
- Chevrolet of Watsonville (tweet dated 17 Dec 2023): a user instructed the dealership's ChatGPT-powered site chatbot to agree with anything the customer said and end each reply with "that's a legally binding offer - no takesies backsies", then got it to "agree" to sell a 2024 Chevy Tahoe for $1. No sale happened; the bot was not authorised to make deals; the vendor was Fullpath; no confidential dealer data was disclosed. Confidence: high. — [AI Incident Database report](https://incidentdatabase.ai/reports/3520); [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/ai/a-chevy-for-1-car-dealer-chatbots-show-perils-of-ai-for-customer-service); [GM Authority](https://gmauthority.com/blog/2023/12/gm-dealer-chat-bot-agrees-to-sell-2024-chevy-tahoe-for-1/amp/)
- DPD (posted 18 Jan 2024): a customer got the parcel firm's chatbot to swear, write a poem about how useless it was and call DPD the "worst delivery firm in the world". DPD blamed an error after a system update and disabled the AI element. Confidence: high. — [ITV News](https://itv.com/news/2024-01-19/dpd-disables-ai-chatbot-after-customer-service-bot-appears-to-go-rogue); [AI Incident Database](https://incidentdatabase.ai/reports/3616)
- Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149 (Feb 2024): the airline's chatbot wrongly said a bereavement fare could be claimed retroactively. The British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal found negligent misrepresentation, rejected Air Canada's argument that the chatbot was responsible for its own actions, held it makes no difference whether information comes from a static page or a chatbot, and awarded $812.02 (refund, interest, fees). It is a small-claims decision, not binding precedent. Confidence: high. — [American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business%5Flaw/resources/business-law-today/2024-february/bc-tribunal-confirms-companies-remain-liable-information-provided-ai-chatbot/); [Law360 Canada](https://www.law360.ca/ca/articles/1804075); [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/aircanada-chatbot-discount-customer)

Benchmarks and red-team studies
- StakeBench (2026; Nanyang Technological University, ST Engineering, IBM Research, UIUC), as reported in press: 3,168 adversarial runs over 264 cases on two browser-agent frameworks (NanoBrowser, BrowserUse) with GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5 Flash. Indirect injections hidden in ordinary web content succeeded roughly 41.7% to 68.2% of the time; direct injections exceeded 79%. Seller-targeted attacks had the highest success. Injected text in product reviews biased the agent toward a product; a manipulated product image raised one product's selection rate from 10% to about 77% in preliminary tests. Swapping models changed indirect success by up to about 26 percentage points. Confidence: medium (press coverage, paper not read). — [CSO Online](https://www.csoonline.com/article/4184455/prompt-injection-breaks-todays-ai-agents-study-warns.html); [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/370972/ai-agents-prompt-injection-attacks-research)
- Magentic Marketplace (Microsoft Research with Arizona State University, arXiv 2510.25779, Oct-Nov 2025): frontier models resisted classic psychological tactics (authority, social proof, loss aversion) but remained vulnerable to adversarial prompt injection from other agents in the market. One press report says only Claude Sonnet 4 resisted the manipulation attempts; the paper's own summary is more general. Confidence: medium. — [arXiv paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.25779); [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/?p=347709)
- ACES sandbox, "What Is Your AI Agent Buying?" (Allouah, Besbes, Figueroa, Kanoria, Kumar; arXiv 2508.02630, Aug 2025): a seller-side agent making small, query-targeted edits to product descriptions gained significant market share from buyer agents. This is manipulation without any "injection" at all. Confidence: medium (abstract only). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.02630v3)

State of defences
- "The Attacker Moves Second" (arXiv 2510.09023, Oct 2025): 12 published prompt-injection and jailbreak defences were tested with adaptive attacks; none was robust. Defences showing attack success as low as 1% on static benchmarks went above 95% under search-based adaptive attack. In a human red-team study, participants produced 265 successful attacks against Spotlighting and 178 against Prompt Sandwiching. Confidence: high (primary paper abstract plus commentary). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.09023); [Simon Willison commentary](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/2/new-prompt-injection-papers/)
- Practitioner consensus following that paper favours architecture over filters. Willison calls Meta's "Agents Rule of Two" the best practical advice available in the absence of reliable prompt-injection defences. (The rule, as generally stated: an agent should not, in one session, combine untrusted input, access to private data or sensitive systems, and the ability to change state or communicate externally without a human in the loop.) Confidence: medium. — [Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/2/new-prompt-injection-papers/)
- Design-pattern approaches: a paper titled "Design Patterns for Securing LLM Agents" lists action-selector, plan-then-execute, LLM map-reduce, dual-LLM, code-then-execute and context-minimisation. CaMeL uses a custom interpreter that tracks data provenance and blocks tool calls where tainted data reaches control flow; it reports strong results on AgentDojo, but that is a static benchmark, and I found no adaptive-attack results for it. Confidence: low to medium (secondary blog). — [Secondary write-up](https://codex.danielvaughan.com/2026/07/08/out-of-band-prompt-injection-defences-camel-fides-progent-codex-cli-sandbox-approval-hooks/)
- OWASP published the Top 10 for Agentic Applications 2026 on 9 Dec 2025 (codes ASI01 to ASI10, over 100 contributors). ASI01 is Agent Goal Hijack: malicious instructions embedded in content the agent reads. Recommended mitigations include treating all retrieved content as untrusted and requiring human approval before irreversible actions or material goal changes. Supply-chain risk (ASI04) covers forged agent-to-agent instructions over channels such as MCP and A2A. Confidence: medium (vendor summaries of the OWASP document). — [Cycode summary](https://cycode.com/blog/owasp-top-10-agentic-applications/); [SecureW2 summary](https://securew2.com/blog/owasp-top-10-agentic-ai)
- Commentary on Moffatt notes Air Canada might have fared better had the chatbot warned that its information might be inaccurate; the tribunal's finding was failure to take reasonable care to ensure accuracy. Confidence: medium. — [Manatt](https://manatt.com/insights/newsletters/advertising-law/ai-gone-wild-airline-has-to-honor-a-refund-policy)

### Inferences
- What works (supported by the evidence above): price floors, discount caps, stock and refund rules enforced in ordinary code that the model cannot override; the model proposes a structured action (quote, hold, order) and a rules layer accepts or rejects it; human confirmation for anything irreversible or above a value threshold; allow-lists of tools, recipients and payment destinations; treating every inbound message, product page, review, image and other-agent message as data, never as instructions.
- What does not work alone: system-prompt instructions ("never go below X"), input/output filter models, delimiter or "spotlighting" tricks, and reliance on a stronger model. They reduce casual abuse but fail under adaptive attack.
- The $1 Tahoe and DPD cases cost reputation, not money, only because the bots could not execute anything. Once an agent can commit to a price or take payment, the same attack becomes a financial loss, and Moffatt suggests the business will own it.
- A negotiating agent must assume the counterparty (human or agent) is adversarial by default. Seller-targeted attacks being the most successful in StakeBench matters for a small-seller agent in particular.

### Gaps
- I found no public, verified incident of a deployed negotiating agent losing real money to prompt injection; evidence is from benchmarks and pranks.
- "Agent session smuggling" and other named A2A-protocol attacks: my search returned no description; not verified.
- I did not find quantitative evidence on how much each individual safeguard (e.g. human confirmation) reduces loss in production.
- StakeBench and design-pattern paper details are from secondary coverage; exact figures should be checked against the papers.

## 2. Agent-to-agent market experiments: bias, collusion, deception, unequal outcomes

### Takeaway
Agents in simulated and small real markets show strong, non-human biases (first offer wins 60-100% of the time; position effects that differ by model), tend toward tacit price collusion, and give worse results to whoever has the weaker model, often without that person noticing. All evidence is from simulations or small pilots, so directions are reliable and magnitudes are not.

### Cited Findings
- Magentic Marketplace (Microsoft Research / ASU, arXiv 2510.25779; experiments with 100 customer agents and 300 business agents in a restaurant setting): first proposals were selected 60-100% of the time versus near zero for third proposals, described as a 10-30x advantage to the business that responds first. Agents appear to accept the first acceptable offer instead of comparing. Position bias in search listings was mild in frontier models and severe in smaller open-source models (some favoured the last listed business). Coverage also reports that giving agents more options did not improve outcomes. Confidence: high for direction, medium for exact figures. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.25779); [startuphub.ai coverage](https://startuphub.ai/news/ai-agent-marketplaces-face-critical-flaws-microsoft-research-finds)
- ACES (arXiv 2508.02630, Aug 2025): all tested shopping agents favoured the top row but different models preferred different columns; effects persisted in text-only interfaces. Agents consistently penalised "sponsored" tags and rewarded platform endorsements. Sensitivity to price, ratings and reviews was human-like in direction but varied sharply in size across models. Demand concentrated on a few "modal" products, and a model update could drastically reshuffle market shares. Confidence: medium (abstract and summaries). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.02630v3); [Hugging Face paper page](https://huggingface.co/papers/2508.02630)
- "The Automated but Risky Game" (arXiv 2506.00073, first posted May 2025): in simulated buyer-seller negotiation over electronics, vehicles and real estate, stronger models extracted better deals from weaker ones; buyers with weaker agents paid around 2% more; within a model family larger models did better in both roles; GPT-3.5 lagged badly. The paper also documents agent failure modes that act against the principal (e.g. overspending budget, accepting bad deals). A blog claims seller profit loss of up to 14%; I could not verify that figure. Limitation noted by an outside academic: simulations may not capture real negotiation. Confidence: medium. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00073v4); [Stanford HAI](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/the-art-of-the-automated-negotiation); [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2025/06/17/1118910/ai-price-negotiation/amp/)
- Anthropic "Project Deal" (run Dec 2025, published 24 Apr 2026; secondary coverage only): Claude agents bought and sold real items for 69 employees for one week; 186 deals over 500+ listings, just over $4,000 total. Two runs used only Opus 4.5 and two mixed Opus 4.5 with Haiku 4.5. People represented by the weaker model got worse prices and reportedly did not realise it; differences in deal completion were small. Anthropic called it a pilot with a self-selected pool and said the setup does not reflect how agents should be deployed. Headlines saying the stronger model "won every time" overstate it. Confidence: medium. — [PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/?p=3684931); [Legal IT Insider](https://legaltechnology.com/anthropics-ai-agent-to-agent-marketplace-experiment-the-legal-frameworks-dont-exist/); [Digital Commerce 360](https://www.digitalcommerce360.com/2026/04/30/ecommerce-trends-what-anthropic-openai-and-google-are-each-doing-in-agentic-commerce/)
- Collusion: Fish, Gonczarowski and Shorrer (arXiv 2404.00806, 2024): GPT-4 pricing agents told only to maximise long-run profit quickly and autonomously reached supracompetitive prices in oligopoly settings; small wording changes in the prompt substantially changed the degree. A Jan 2026 theory paper (arXiv 2601.01279) adds that when sellers delegate to the same LLM the result resembles tacit collusion with occasional low prices providing deniability. Conflicting evidence: a consultancy write-up reports smaller experiments where more advanced models were less likely to reach supracompetitive prices. Confidence: medium. — [arXiv 2404.00806](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.00806v4); [arXiv 2601.01279](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.01279v1); [Keystone](https://www.keystone.com/news-publications/policing-an-invisible-handshake)
- Deception and loyalty: 2026 preprints test whether agents withhold, mislead or deceive in bargaining (arXiv 2605.31445) and whether an agent stays loyal to its principal in multi-party settings (arXiv 2606.30383, "Whose Side Is Your Agent On?"). I have titles and summary lines only, no headline numbers. Confidence: low. — [arXiv 2605.31445](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.31445); [arXiv 2606.30383](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.30383)

### Inferences
- Speed of response is a ranking factor in agent markets: a small seller's agent that answers first wins disproportionately. That is a product advantage and also an abuse vector (spam first offers).
- If many sellers use the same model and prompt for pricing, a platform may be accused of facilitating tacit collusion. Per-seller independent price rules set by the owner, not a shared pricing brain, reduce that exposure.
- Unequal model strength means a platform serving one side should disclose which side it serves and should not quietly degrade the model for lower tiers in negotiation, since users cannot see the loss.
- Buyer agents' distrust of "sponsored" labels and reward for platform endorsements means verified badges may carry more weight with agents than ads do.

### Gaps
- No large-scale field data on agent-to-agent commerce with real money beyond the 69-person pilot.
- No India-specific agent-market experiment found.
- Deception and principal-loyalty preprints: numbers not retrieved.
- Anthropic's primary write-up was not opened; figures are from press.

## 3. Fraud and scams, with India numbers

### Takeaway
India's reported cyber-fraud losses are about Rs 22,500 crore a year (2024 and 2025), dominated by investment scams; UPI fraud is about 10-13 lakh incidents and Rs 800-1,100 crore a year. Regulators have responded by removing risky features (person-to-person collect requests ended 1 Oct 2025), scoring phone numbers for fraud risk, and shifting burden of proof to banks; a seller-agent product will be judged against that backdrop.

### Inferences
- The removal of P2P collect requests shows the regulator's pattern: when a feature lets a stranger pull money with one tap, it gets removed. An agent that sends payment requests or QR codes to consumers on behalf of small sellers sits close to that pattern and should use merchant-verified rails only, never personal VPAs or agent-generated arbitrary QR images.
- A badge is not enough: impersonators have appeared with blue ticks and business labels. Buyers need a way to verify the seller out-of-band (platform-hosted profile, registration number lookup).
- Controls a platform should have from day one, inferred from what regulators and WhatsApp already do: verify each business before its agent can message strangers; payee name shown must match the verified business; per-day caps on new conversations, payment links and order value for new sellers, rising with history; check phone numbers against the fraud-risk indicator where access is available; block or rewrite outbound links to an allow-list; one-tap report in every chat; fast freeze and takedown; retain logs for law-enforcement requests.
- AI makes the classic scripts cheaper (fake customer care, fake order confirmation, advance-fee, fake storefront). A seller-agent platform is attractive to scammers precisely because it supplies a polite, tireless, plausibly branded front.

### Gaps
- I found no reliable statistics specifically on scammers using AI chat agents or fake AI storefronts in India; evidence is anecdotal.
- No data retrieved on QR-code scam volumes, fake e-commerce site counts, or fake-review prevalence in India; nothing retrieved on the BIS online-review standard or Central Consumer Protection Authority dark-pattern actions (search returned nothing).
- WhatsApp Business / Meta Verified requirements in India and their KYC tiers: not retrieved.
- NPCI's own fraud-to-sales ratios were not retrieved; UPI numbers are from Parliament replies.
- The I4C 2025 totals conflict (Rs 22,495 crore vs Rs 19,813 crore; 21.8 to 28.2 lakh complaints); the primary Parliament reply should be checked.

## 4. Identity and authorisation: "know your agent", mandates, proving who an agent represents

### Takeaway
By 2026 three complementary schemes exist to prove an agent is authorised: Google-originated AP2 (signed mandates as verifiable credentials), Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol (signed agent identity in web requests) and Mastercard Agent Pay with Verifiable Intent (token binding cardholder, agent and scope). India is building its own delegated-limit route on UPI. None of them yet verifies a small seller's agent; that still depends on ordinary business verification.

### Cited Findings
- AP2 (Agent Payments Protocol): uses linked signed mandates, described in 2026 explainers as a Checkout Mandate and a Payment Mandate, each in open and closed forms, carried as SD-JWT verifiable credentials with selective disclosure. It does not move money; it creates a verifiable record any rail (card, bank, stablecoin) can settle against. Governance: one source says the Apache-2.0 repository under google-agentic-commerce; another says it was donated to the FIDO Alliance in April 2026. Confidence: medium (vendor explainers; spec not read; governance claim conflicting). — [eco.com explainer](https://eco.com/support/en/articles/15192002-ap2-protocol-explained-google-s-agentic-commerce-standard-2026); [Spark research note](https://www.spark.money/research/google-ap2-agent-payment-protocol)
- Visa Trusted Agent Protocol: attests agent identity by signing it into HTTP request headers so merchants can tell a recognised agent from a bot. Part of Visa Intelligent Commerce; as of April 2026 described as optional for forward-leaning merchants, with broad adoption not expected before 2027. Confidence: medium. — [Stellagent](https://stellagent.ai/insights/visa-intelligent-commerce-vic-explained)
- Mastercard Agent Pay: issues an Agentic Token through its tokenisation service that binds cardholder, registered agent and mandate scope; a companion "Verifiable Intent" standard tokenises the user's intent. AP2 mandates are designed to cross-reference Visa TAP and Mastercard Verifiable Intent, and both networks have said they are AP2-compatible. Confidence: medium. — [eco.com agent identity article](https://eco.com/support/en/articles/15192005-agent-identity-verification-how-ai-agents-authenticate-purchases-in-2026)
- India: Razorpay and NPCI launched agentic payments on Claude (with Zomato, Swiggy, Zepto) at the India AI Impact Summit in February 2026. Reuters-sourced reports (mid-2026) say NPCI is preparing a "Unified Agent Protocol" letting agents make small UPI payments without per-transaction approval, reusing UPI Circle and Reserve Pay so users delegate within a preset limit; NPCI had not formally confirmed, and a Rs 15,000 cap appears in one explainer without official backing. NPCI launched AiNxt (agent-building) and AtOM (orchestration) platforms at Global Fintech Fest 2026. Pine Labs' agent payment protocol is reported live and Mastercard demonstrated an authenticated agentic transaction with Indian banks. Confidence: high for the Razorpay launch; medium to low for the protocol details. — [Razorpay newsroom](https://razorpay.com/newsroom/razorpay-npci-launch-agentic-payments-on-claude-powering-zomato-swiggy-zepto-at-the-india-ai-impact-summit/); [Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/news/india-plans-ai-powered-upi-payments-framework-through-unified-agent-protocol); [Deccan Chronicle](https://www.deccanchronicle.com/amp/technology/india-preparing-framework-to-let-ai-agents-make-small-upi-payments-without-approval-1983986); [Angel One](https://www.angelone.in/news/economy/npci-launches-ainxt-and-atom-agentic-ai-platforms-for-upi-ecosystem); [Inc42](https://inc42.com/buzz/npci-to-launch-agentic-payments-on-upi-report/)
- Agents in the ACES study rewarded platform endorsements and penalised sponsored tags, which suggests verification badges influence buyer agents as well as humans. Confidence: medium. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.02630v3)

### Inferences
- All three network schemes focus on the buyer side (is this agent authorised by this cardholder, for this scope). Seller-agent identity is left to the platform. A practical ladder for a small Indian seller: phone OTP (weak, proves control of a number only); then match the name on the settlement bank account or merchant VPA to the claimed business; then a government registration checked against the public registry (GST number, Udyam number for micro-enterprises, FSSAI licence for food); then a platform badge that states what was checked and when. This ladder is my inference; I retrieved no source describing a platform doing exactly this.
- The delegated-limit model (preset cap, revocable, logged) is the pattern both the card networks and NPCI are converging on. A product should model every agent authority as a mandate with scope, amount cap, expiry and a signed record, even before the formal protocols reach small merchants.
- Because adoption of the network schemes is expected in 2027 or later, a founder should design the mandate record to be exportable into AP2-style credentials later, and not depend on them now.

### Gaps
- Primary specifications (AP2, Visa TAP, Mastercard Agent Pay) were not opened; details rest on explainers, some vendor-affiliated.
- "Know Your Agent" as a named standard: no authoritative source retrieved.
- No source found on GST / Udyam / FSSAI verification APIs being used for agent identity, or on WhatsApp business verification steps in India.
- NPCI Unified Agent Protocol: no official document found; limits unconfirmed; whether it was actually unveiled at Global Fintech Fest in September 2026 is not confirmed by my sources.

## 5. Liability and disputes

### Takeaway
The deploying business bears the loss when its agent misstates something (Moffatt), and for agent-initiated card purchases the merchant carries the chargeback exposure because no network has published a liability rule specific to agents. Signed mandates and transcripts are the evidence that will decide disputes.

### Cited Findings
- Moffatt v. Air Canada (Feb 2024): business liable for its chatbot's misstatement; "the chatbot is a separate entity" defence rejected; duty of reasonable care to ensure accuracy. Small-claims level. Confidence: high. — [American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business%5Flaw/resources/business-law-today/2024-february/bc-tribunal-confirms-companies-remain-liable-information-provided-ai-chatbot/)
- Chevrolet $1 case: no binding sale resulted; the bot had no authority to conclude deals. Confidence: high. — [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/ai/a-chevy-for-1-car-dealer-chatbots-show-perils-of-ai-for-customer-service)
- Card networks (as of 2026, per industry commentary): Visa TAP, Mastercard Agent Pay, AP2 and related protocols produce signed identity and intent records, but none publishes a dispute-liability allocation. Agent disputes use existing reason codes (cited: Visa 10.4, 13.2, 13.3; Mastercard 4837, 4841, 4853) and count toward Visa's monitoring programme (excessive at 1.5%) and Mastercard's equivalent with no separate allowance. Confidence: medium to low (vendor sources; rulebooks not checked). — [Justt](https://justt.ai/blog/ai-agent-chargeback-liability/); [Chargeflow](https://www.chargeflow.io/blog/ai-agent-chargeback-liability)
- Post-transaction liability is expected to remain concentrated on merchants; the behavioural signals merchants use to win disputes do not exist for agent-initiated purchases. Confidence: medium. — [The Paypers](https://thepaypers.com/fraud-and-fincrime/news/chargebacks911-warns-of-dispute-risk-gap-in-agentic-payments)
- One source says a US consumer finance regulator advisory in January 2026 placed agent-initiated card transactions within the existing dispute regime; the EU's next payments law may address AI-initiated transactions but was still under negotiation. Confidence: low (single vendor source; not verified). — [Chargeflow](https://www.chargeflow.io/blog/agentic-commerce-regulation-what-merchants-need-to-know)
- Private cover is emerging: a chargeback firm reportedly indemnifies merchants against qualifying chargebacks on agent purchases. Mastercard's chief executive has publicly raised the question of who is liable when AI spends a customer's money. Confidence: low to medium. — [Stellagent on indemnification](https://stellagent.ai/insights/chargebacks911-ai-agent-purchase-indemnification); [Stellagent on Mastercard](https://stellagent.ai/insights/mastercard-ceo-agentic-commerce-risks)
- Anthropic's Project Deal write-up prompted legal commentary that the legal frameworks for agent-to-agent deals do not yet exist. Confidence: medium. — [Legal IT Insider](https://legaltechnology.com/anthropics-ai-agent-to-agent-marketplace-experiment-the-legal-frameworks-dont-exist/)
- India: under the revised RBI framework the bank must prove the customer was at fault in unauthorised electronic transactions, with a 30-day resolution clock. Confidence: medium. — [Outlook Money](https://www.outlookmoney.com/banking/who-pays-when-a-digital-fraud-takes-place-rbi-shifts-burden-of-proof-to-banks)

### Inferences
- For a small-seller agent: the seller (and reputationally the platform) owns wrong price, wrong stock and wrong delivery promises. The design consequence is that the agent should quote only from the seller's own price list and stock record, and the order becomes binding only after an explicit confirmation step showing item, price, address and total.
- Whether an agent-approved purchase counts as "authorised" or "unauthorised" under Indian rules is unsettled; an auditable mandate (who delegated what, with what cap, when) is the likely deciding evidence. This is inference, not a cited rule.
- Minimum dispute kit: immutable transcript, the structured order object the customer confirmed, timestamped confirmation, the agent's instruction set and limits at that time, and a short cancel window before fulfilment for agent-taken orders.

### Gaps
- No primary network rulebook text or regulator document on agent liability retrieved.
- No Indian court or consumer-commission decision on chatbot or agent statements found.
- No insurer product specifically for agent errors confirmed beyond one vendor indemnity claim.
- No evidence on how existing agent products implement cool-off periods, or how often confirmation steps prevent disputes.

## 6. Disclosure and consumer trust

### Takeaway
Disclosure is legally required or the safe default in the EU and California, and the best field experiment shows up-front disclosure can cut sales sharply (over 79.7% in one 2019 study), though timing and prior AI experience soften this. Indian consumers report the highest openness to AI-assisted shopping, yet globally only about 9% let an AI buy on its own.

### Cited Findings

Effect of disclosure
- Luo, Tong, Fang and Qu, Marketing Science 2019: field experiment with over 6,200 customers of one financial-services firm receiving outbound sales calls. Undisclosed chatbots sold as well as proficient human workers and about four times better than inexperienced ones; disclosing the bot's identity before the conversation cut purchase rates by more than 79.7%, because customers saw the disclosed bot as less knowledgeable and less empathetic. Late disclosure and prior AI experience mitigated the effect. Caveats: 2019, voice calls, one company, pre-generative-AI. Confidence: high for the result; medium for present-day applicability. — [Marketing Science record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:38:y:2019:i:6:p:937-947); [Temple University summary](https://www.fox.temple.edu/news/2020/01/chatbots-can-help-improve-salesprovided-you-dont-reveal-its-chatbot)
- Schilke and Reimann, "The Transparency Dilemma" (Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 2025): 13 experiments, over 5,000 participants; disclosing AI use lowered trust, softer framings still lowered it, and the drop was larger when AI use was exposed by someone else than when self-disclosed. Confidence: high. — [University of Arizona](https://news.arizona.edu/news/disclosing-ai-use-can-backfire-research-shows)

Law
- EU AI Act Article 50: people must be told they are interacting with an AI system unless obvious; several sources give 2 Aug 2026 as the date the duty applies. Confidence: medium (compliance blogs; regulation text not opened; any later delay to the timetable not checked). — [CX Network](https://www.cxnetwork.com/artificial-intelligence/articles/chatbot-regulations-ab-1609-eu-ai-act); [JD Supra](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/ai-trends-for-2026-return-of-the-6382428/)
- California: the older bot-disclosure law (section 17941, in force since 2019) makes it unlawful to use a bot to mislead about its artificial identity for a commercial or electoral purpose, with clear disclosure as a safe harbour, so operators disclose by default. SB 243 (2025) targets companion chatbots and requires clear notice that the bot is not human; sources say it excludes bots used only for customer service. A further bill, AB 1609, is discussed in sources but I did not retrieve its content. Confidence: medium. — [Training Camp](https://trainingcamp.com/articles/california-ai-chatbot-laws-bot-act-sb-243-and-ab-1609/); [CX Network](https://www.cxnetwork.com/artificial-intelligence/articles/chatbot-regulations-ab-1609-eu-ai-act)
- India: the 2026 IT Rules amendment requires labels on synthetically generated content (in force 20 Feb 2026). I found no Indian rule that specifically requires a customer-service or sales chatbot to announce that it is an AI. Confidence: high for the label rule; the absence finding is low confidence because the search returned nothing on point. — [Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/technology/story/govt-tightens-rules-on-ai-generated-content-mandates-labels-and-faster-takedowns-515505-2026-02-10)

Willingness to delegate
- Ipsos (global, mid-2026): only 9% of consumers currently let AI make autonomous purchases and 24% would consider it. Confidence: medium. — [Ipsos Views: Shopping with AI](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/publication/documents/2026-07/ipsos-views-shopping-with-AI.pdf)
- Visa (Asia Pacific, Feb 2026): 74% already use AI to shop; India and Vietnam led with 42% open to using AI for online purchases; trust and transparency gaps stall checkout. Confidence: medium. — [Financial IT](https://financialit.net/news/artificial-intelligence/74-asia-pacific-consumers-already-use-ai-shop-trust-and-transparency); [Marketech APAC](https://marketech-apac.com/around-74-of-apac-shoppers-use-ai-but-trust-gaps-stall-checkout/)
- Adobe (2026): 58% of Indian consumers are comfortable with agent-to-agent interactions; 61% are happy for an AI agent to deal with a brand's human representative for them; 65% of Indian shoppers trust AI recommendations, highest in Asia Pacific. Confidence: medium. — [Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/technology/artificial-intelligence/65-of-indian-shoppers-trust-ai-recommendations-highest-in-asia-pacific-adobe-report-4061340); [Lapaas Voice](https://voice.lapaas.com/india-leads-ai-agent-race-adobe-report/)
- Accenture Consumer Pulse 2026: India figures reported as 94% wanting to shop inside generative-AI tools and 86% likely to follow such tools for half their spend decisions; a claim that 90% of Indians trust AI agents over their best friend conflicts with the global figure of about 74% across 16 countries. Confidence: low to medium (vendor survey, online panels). — [CXOToday](https://cxotoday.com/?p=236040); [Accenture report](https://www.accenture.com/content/dam/accenture/final/accenture-com/document-fy26/q4/Accenture-Consumer-Pulse-2026.pdf)
- McKinsey (June 2026): 76% of Indian consumers use AI shopping tools (second globally), 87% trust AI chat and search platforms; millennials lead at 84%. This measures trust in AI search, not delegated buying. Confidence: medium. — [BW Retail World](https://bwretailworld.com/fmcg/millennials-lead-ai-shopping-adoption-in-india-at-84-ahead-of-gen-z-mckinsey)

### Inferences
- Disclosure is not optional for a product that may reach EU or California users, and concealment carries legal and reputational risk in any market. The evidence argues for disclosing clearly but pairing it with competence signals (named business, accurate answers, quick human handover), since the sales penalty came from perceived low knowledge and empathy.
- Indian survey numbers come from online, urban, English-comfortable panels and consulting-firm samples; they probably overstate readiness among small-town buyers and the customers of very small sellers.
- The gap between "use AI to research" (70% plus) and "let AI buy" (about 9%) says checkout authority is the trust bottleneck. Keeping the human as the one who confirms payment fits both the trust data and the security findings in section 1.
- Over-trust is also a risk: Project Deal participants with the weaker model did not notice worse prices, so users cannot be relied on to detect a poorly performing agent.

### Gaps
- No post-2023 field experiment on disclosure and conversion for generative-AI chat agents found; the 79.7% figure predates them.
- No India-specific study of disclosure effects; no explicit Indian consumer-protection guidance on bot disclosure located.
- Survey questions differ, so the India figures (42%, 58%, 61%, 65%, 86%, 87%, 94%) are not comparable to each other.
- EU Article 50 timing and California AB 1609 content should be confirmed against the legal texts.

## 7. Privacy and data leakage

### Takeaway
Negotiating agents leak their principal's private limits, both by stating them when asked and through the pattern of their concessions; 2025-2026 benchmarks show agents that optimise for closing deals leak more and get worse outcomes for the user. The dependable mitigation is to keep secrets out of the model's context and enforce them in code.

### Cited Findings
- "Whose Side Is Your Agent On?" (arXiv 2606.30383, 2026): benchmark cases include whether a sales agent reveals a used-car seller's $12,000 floor, $11,500 walk-away threshold and motivation to sell when the buyer simply asks for the lowest acceptable price. Confidence: medium (scenario confirmed; result numbers not retrieved). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.30383)
- MAGPIE (arXiv 2510.15186, Oct 2025): multi-agent benchmark where private information is tied to task success across negotiation, admissions and resource allocation; it reports the percentage of private information leaked by each model during negotiation. Confidence: medium (numbers not retrieved). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.15186); [project page](https://jaypasnagasai.github.io/magpie)
- SovereignNegotiation-Bench (arXiv 2607.02814, 2026): for personal agents negotiating on a user's behalf, a deal-maximising agent reached more agreements but leaked more information and delivered lower utility than a privacy-respecting one; revealing a reservation price to speed up a sale is a listed failure. Uses the contextual-integrity framing (information should flow only where appropriate). Confidence: medium. — [alphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.02814)
- Behavioural leakage (arXiv 2607.06815, 2026, attributed to Apple researchers): an opponent can infer private constraints from concession paths, timing and convergence without the agent stating them. A randomised policy with differential-privacy guarantees cut the adversary's inference accuracy by 43-50% while keeping a 90.4% negotiation success rate. Confidence: medium. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.06815)
- On-device negotiation agents are proposed as a way to keep the principal's data local (arXiv 2601.00911). Confidence: low (title-level). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.00911)
- The Chevrolet chatbot incident did not expose confidential dealer data, per the vendor, which shows the value of not giving a public bot access to such data in the first place. Confidence: medium. — [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/ai/a-chevy-for-1-car-dealer-chatbots-show-perils-of-ai-for-customer-service)
- Meta's Rule of Two, as endorsed in practitioner commentary, treats access to private data as one of three properties that should not all be present in a session that also reads untrusted input and can act externally. Confidence: medium. — [Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/2/new-prompt-injection-papers/)

### Inferences
- Do not put the seller's floor price in the prompt. Let the model propose an offer and have a rules layer answer accept, reject or counter; the model then cannot reveal what it never saw. The same applies to a buyer's maximum budget.
- Cross-customer leakage is prevented structurally: one conversation context per customer, retrieval scoped by customer and seller identifiers in code, no shared memory across buyers, and no free-text recall of other orders.
- Concession patterns leak too, so fixed, predictable step-downs let a patient counterparty (especially another agent probing repeatedly) map the floor. Limit the number of counter-offers per buyer and add variation.
- Data minimisation helps with India's data protection obligations as well as security: collect address and phone only at order confirmation, and keep transcripts with a stated retention period. I did not retrieve sources on the Indian data-protection rules, so this is inference.

### Gaps
- Leak-rate numbers from MAGPIE and the loyalty benchmark were not retrieved.
- No documented real-world incident of a commerce agent leaking cross-customer data found in this search.
- Nothing retrieved on India's Digital Personal Data Protection rules as applied to chat agents.
- All privacy papers cited are preprints; peer-review status unknown.

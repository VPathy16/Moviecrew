# Different Briefs, Not More Agents, Widen Brainstorms

The two sessions collapsed into a dozen themes because every agent received the same brief, and sending one prompt to many independent sessions is the least diverse way of using a language model that has been measured. In the largest 2026 product-idea study, one idea per independent session from a shared prompt scored **0.452** on a diversity scale where the same model listing 100 ideas in one session scored 0.679 and a pool of human ideas 0.773. Thirty-three copies of one model behave like one person asked 33 times, not like 33 people, so the step from 10 to 33 agents mostly bought repeats. The best-supported fix is to give each agent a different, task-relevant starting point (an ordinary user persona, one hard constraint, or one cell of a map of the idea space drawn up beforehand) and to have each agent draft a list, revise it for difference and only then write up. Per-agent personas and constraints lifted the same scale to **0.757 and 0.735**, while the whole temperature range from 0 to 1 moved it by 0.06; example ideas, one "do not repeat" list shared by everyone and free discussion between agents all pull agents together. After a blind first round, two further short rounds beat one large one: the coordinator deduplicates the pool, lays it on a small grid, and sends agents to the empty cells with one or two parent ideas and a named way of changing them, adding new ideas without overwriting old ones. Models can deduplicate, label and discard clearly broken ideas, but on "is this idea new and good" they agree with experts at about chance (**53.3% against 56.1%** between human reviewers), so two or three people should make coarse keep-or-drop calls on one representative per group and reserve a few places for unusual ideas. The evidence has two large holes: no study tests a few dozen small-model agents on product ideas, and most diversity gains are measured as embedding distance, not as value judged by people. The protocol at the end therefore includes a same-brief control arm and a fixed scorecard, so the next session is also the team's own experiment.

## One brief sent to 33 agents is the least diverse design measured

The team's set-up has a name in the research: independent sessions with an identical prompt. Terwiesch and colleagues at Wharton (July 2026) measured the diversity of pools of 100 product ideas from GPT-4o under ten conditions, and this one ranked last. One idea per independent session scored **0.452** on diversity (1 minus the mean pairwise cosine similarity of the ideas' embeddings), against 0.679 for 100 ideas listed in one session and 0.773 for ideas written by separate people ([Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553)). If the team's agents each returned several ideas, the sessions sit somewhere between those two model figures, but the mechanism is the same. A Stanford study generated 4,000 seed research ideas per topic and found only about **200 (5%)** that were not duplicates of an earlier one, with the share of new ideas falling in every batch, even though each prompt carried the titles of all earlier ideas and an instruction not to repeat them ([Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04109)). In a GPT-4 study of about 1,200 product ideas, the commonest ideas each appeared 25 to 30 times and "often occur early in a completion request" ([Meincke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01727)). Many short parallel runs therefore each return the head of the same distribution. The effect is not peculiar to one vendor: across more than 70 models, 50 responses to the same open-ended query had average pairwise similarity above 0.8 in 79% of cases, and responses from different models were 71% to 82% similar to each other ([Jiang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.22954)).

The best-supported explanation has three parts. First, a model pools everyone's knowledge into one central distribution, whereas a human population is partitioned: each person holds different knowledge. Deng, Brucks and Toubia at Columbia report that the first ideas of independent GPT-4o sessions covered far fewer hand-coded categories than the first ideas of independent people (about 8.7 against 14.3), while fixation on earlier ideas within a session was about the same for the model and for people ([Deng et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20408)). The human advantage sits between sessions, which is exactly where a parallel brainstorm needs variety. Second, training for helpfulness rewards typical answers: in 6,874 response pairs matched for correctness, human raters preferred the more typical response 51.6% to 60.8% of the time, and that preference is what models are tuned on ([Zhang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01171)). Third, an identical prompt puts every run at the same starting point, and material in the prompt narrows it further: six good example ideas made a GPT-4 pool slightly less diverse than no examples (similarity 0.428 against 0.414) ([Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553)). Anthropic's guide to agent patterns separates two kinds of parallel work: "sectioning", where agents take different sub-tasks, and "voting", where the same task runs several times ([Anthropic: Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)). The two sessions were voting. A brainstorm needs sectioning.

Read against that evidence, almost every feature of the two sessions pushed agents together, as the table shows.

| Feature of the two sessions | What the evidence says | Replacement |
|---|---|---|
| Same brief of about 400 words to every agent | Identical prompts were last of ten conditions (0.452) ([Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553)) | A short shared core plus a different assignment per agent |
| Shared background and three fixed rules | Text every agent shares cannot separate agents; shared examples and articles made pools slightly less diverse ([Meincke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01727)). No study tests a long shared brief directly | Keep only the goal, true limits and the output format |
| One "do not repeat these" list for all | Prior titles in every prompt still left 95% duplicates ([Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04109)); showing every call the same items to avoid was the weakest of the anti-repeat methods compared ([Ibrahim et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.30150)) | Short theme labels, plus slices that already point elsewhere |
| One open question | In people, a narrow problem produced more original ideas than a broad one, with no loss in number ([Rietzschel et al. 2014](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/effects-of-problem-scope-and-creativity-instructions-on-idea-gene/)) | One narrow slice per agent |
| "Imagine freely" | A "very creative" system prompt raised population variability from 0.459 to 0.576, against 0.738 for people ([Wenger & Kenett 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.19361)) | One plain sentence, with structure doing the work |
| One round | Idea systems gain over about three rounds of review and refill, then flatten ([Hu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.14255)) | Three rounds |
| One person grouping by hand | Human reviewers of ideas agree with each other only 56.1% of the time ([Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109)) | Machine deduplication checked by two or three people |
| Scaling from 10 to 33 agents | Coverage improves slowly with volume: growing a pool from 100 to 1,000 ideas cut the mean distance to the nearest of 200 human ideas only from 0.546 to 0.478 ([Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553)) | Structure first, head-count second |

One caution belongs here. The dozen themes are not worthless. Model ideas concentrate "in regions of the idea space that are commercially plausible but less novel", and human ideas that sat far from any model idea had lower purchase intent ([Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553)). In human idea pools, too, the least similar ideas "are not generally the most valuable ones" ([Kornish & Ulrich 2011](https://repository.upenn.edu/fnce_papers/104)). The dozen themes are banked. The job of the next session is to find what lies outside them and then to check whether any of it is good.

## Per-agent personas, slices and constraints out-pull every shared instruction

The levers with the largest measured gains all do one of two things: they give each run a different starting point, or they make a run look at its own earlier ideas and work against them. The one study that tested many levers on a single scale gives the clearest ranking. Scores from different studies cannot be compared with these, because each study uses its own embedding model, task and scale.

| Condition (GPT-4o, product ideas, pools of 100; higher is more diverse) | Diversity |
|---|---|
| Humans | 0.773 |
| Balanced pool from four vendors' models | 0.763 |
| Random ordinary persona per session (one of 100 students) | 0.757 |
| A person picks the problem, the model proposes solutions | 0.739 |
| Random functional constraint per session (one of 20) | 0.735 |
| Draft a list, make the items more different, then describe | 0.698 |
| Random Shakespeare sonnet placed before the prompt | 0.686 |
| Plain prompt, 100 ideas per session, temperature 1 | 0.679 |
| Plain prompt, temperature 0.5 / 0 | 0.626 / 0.618 |
| "Make this idea bolder", applied twice to each idea separately | 0.575 |
| One idea per independent session | 0.452 |

Source: [Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553).

### Ordinary personas and planned slices do the separating

A different ordinary persona per agent was the best single-model lever on the Wharton scale (0.757 against 0.679), and the Columbia group found the same on hand-coded categories: ordinary personas brought the number of category combinations up to the human level and beat curated "creative" personas ([Deng et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20408)). Stranger is not better. Near-identical personas were worst, varied ordinary students best, and outlandish personas such as a Mars colonist were significantly worse than ordinary ones; their ideas moved away from human ideas but still clustered together ([Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553)). Famous-innovator personas applied to a whole pool barely move it (Steve Jobs 0.368 against a base of 0.377 in similarity) ([Meincke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01727)). Personas must go one per agent: five professional personas in five separate prompts gave more diverse design concepts than no persona, while putting all five in one prompt gave the lowest diversity of the three approaches ([Feng et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/dsj.2025.10037)). The persona result has one counter-example worth knowing. In a re-analysis of a 2024 business-ideas dataset, GPT-4 pools with a random persona were more similar than pools without one (0.56 against 0.465) ([Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553)). In interacting groups, interdisciplinary expert personas gave the least diverse pools of all, below no personas, while flat groups of junior peers gave the most ([Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.18005v2)). The safe reading is: personas of ordinary, varied users on the generating agents, and expertise on the critics.

The second way to separate agents is to split the space before anyone generates. In a May 2026 study on three current models, one planning call proposed five semantic directions and the parallel calls were divided evenly among them. With one added line asking each answer to stand out from what others would write, mean pairwise distance rose by **0.13 to 0.25** over plain independent calls at 1.61 times the tokens; the "stand out" line alone added 0.04 to 0.06. A method that showed each call its own earlier output plus one or two peers did about as well but cost 3.71 times the tokens ([Ibrahim et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.30150)). No second group has replicated this yet, and quality was scored by automatic proxies. A full grid of slices (user by situation by mechanism, say) rests on adjacent evidence only: narrow problem statements raise originality in people ([Rietzschel et al. 2014](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/effects-of-problem-scope-and-creativity-instructions-on-idea-gene/)), Anthropic's research system cured duplicated work by giving subagents "clearly divided responsibilities" ([Anthropic engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)), and the grid-based search methods described later all found that agents must be given a target cell. No experiment compares a pre-assigned grid against an open brief for LLM ideation.

### One hard constraint per agent, and randomness that is about the task

A different functional constraint per session was worth nearly as much as a persona (0.735). The same study graded constraints on five tiers: "extreme but physically possible" constraints (the product must fit in a walnut shell) gave the most diverse pools of any tier and came closest to human ideas, surreal impossible ones (made of moonlight) lost the gain, and mild practical ones helped least ([Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553)). Human research describes an inverted U: across 145 empirical studies people and teams "benefit from a healthy dose of constraints", and creativity suffers only when constraints become too many ([Acar et al., HBR 2019](https://hbr.org/2019/11/why-constraints-are-good-for-innovation)). The falling side shows up in models of the size the team uses: as the number of story constraints rose to 39, quality fell for every model tested, most of them at 7B scale ([Atmakuru et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.04197)). One hard constraint per agent is the supported form. A stack of several is not.

Randomness helps only when it bears on the task. A random sonnet in front of the prompt moved the Wharton scale from 0.679 to 0.686, while random personas and constraints moved it roughly ten times as far; the authors conclude that "the type of introduced heterogeneity matters" ([Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553)). Random seed words have support only from list-recall tasks, where one random word reportedly raised the median count of unique items over 100 runs from 22 to 32 on a 4B model ([Agrawal & Goyal 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18053)). Analogies from distant fields, the classic human technique, are weaker for models than their reputation suggests. In a 2026 experiment with 140 people and seven LLMs, a random source domain raised human originality but gave strong models no gain across all pairs, a modest gain for the most distant half of pairs and a larger one for the most distant tenth ([Liu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.19087)). In research ideation, seeds retrieved from other domains beat no retrieval but only matched a control of random diverse seeds ([Choi & Song 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11532)). Far sources also trade usefulness for novelty in people: solvers from analogous markets gave more novel but less immediately usable solutions ([Franke et al. 2014](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2013.1805)), and in 12 open-innovation challenges the ideas experts shortlisted more often cited near sources ([Chan et al. 2014](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~spdow/files/conceptualDistance-CSCW2014.pdf)). Far analogy therefore suits a minority of agents, with sources that are truly remote and an instruction to carry over how the source works, not how it looks.

Named creativity methods such as SCAMPER, TRIZ or reversing assumptions are best treated as routing devices. A 2026 benchmark of 25 method-inspired prompts on five models found that all of them beat control prompts, that "no single method simultaneously maximizes idea fluency, novelty, rarity and diversity", and that novelty gains tracked how explicit the instruction to diverge was more than the structure of the method. TRIZ-style contradiction prompts and a prompt that has the model categorise its ideas and then generate outside those categories scored best at the top end; SCAMPER did not reliably raise novelty; and method prompts roughly halved the number of ideas per call ([IDEAFix 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.00875)). The benchmark used embedding metrics and no human judges. In people, creativity training as a whole has an effect of about 0.3 standard deviations once publication bias is corrected ([Psychological Bulletin meta-analysis, 2024](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/209749/)). A method will not make any one agent much more creative. Its use is to send that agent somewhere its neighbours are not going.

### Each agent drafts a list, revises it for difference, then writes up

Asking for many ideas in one call beats one idea per call by a wide margin (0.679 against 0.452), because the model can see what it has already said. Adding an explicit revision step helps further. The original version (list short titles, revise the list to make the ideas "bolder and more different", then describe them) was the best of 35 prompts in 2024, at 0.255 mean similarity against 0.377 for the base prompt and 0.243 for a human pool, though that comparison also changed model version ([Meincke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01727)). On GPT-4o in 2026 the gain was modest (0.698 against 0.679) ([Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553)). The Columbia study reports a clear gain on categories and, more usefully, an interaction: personas spread sessions apart but slightly increase fixation inside each session, the revision step reduces fixation, and the two combined produced the most diverse pools of any condition for every model tested, at or above the human pool ([Deng et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20408)). Two practical details matter. About 15% of runs in the 2024 study skipped the revision step and had to be discarded, so the coordinator must check that it happened ([Meincke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01727)). And the revision has to apply to the list as a whole: telling the model to "make this idea bolder" one idea at a time lowered diversity to 0.575, because every idea drifted toward the same "novel" features, notably scents ([Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553)).

A related technique asks the model for several candidates together with their probabilities. Its authors report 1.6 to 2.1 times the diversity of direct prompting in creative writing, but gains grow with model size and less capable models can lose output quality ([Zhang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01171)). A method that has the model write a random string and derive its answer from it carries a warning that models under about 8B parameters may fail to carry it out ([Misaki & Akiba 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.21150v3)). For a fan-out on small models, persona and constraint assignment need no reasoning ability and are the safer choice. That is an inference: the only persona study that included a small model reports direction, not size ([Feng et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/dsj.2025.10037)).

### Examples, shared avoid-lists and creativity advice come out of the brief

Example ideas anchor. Seven successful examples made GPT-4 pools less diverse than the base prompt (0.428 against 0.377 in similarity), as did supplying previous top ideas (0.403), a Harvard Business Review article on brainstorming (0.387) or a Stanford design-thinking article (0.392). Tipping, threats and politeness changed nothing ([Meincke et al. 2024](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/for-web-AI-idea-variance.pdf)). The same holds for people: in a meta-analysis of 43 design studies, examples led to more example-related ideas and fewer categories ([Sio et al. 2015](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100081707150_0x000001)).

Avoid-lists need more care than they got. Banning what came before does raise novelty, but in the one study that pushed it hard (on coding problems) the share of techniques never seen in human solutions rose from 4.5% to 15.3% while the pass rate fell from 16.1% to 2.1%, and the combined score peaked after a single round of denial ([Lu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.09007)). A list that is the same for every agent removes the named ideas and leaves every agent standing at the same next-most-likely ideas. Two better forms exist. One is to describe what is covered as abstract category labels, which is the logic of the "generate outside these categories" prompt that scored well above ([IDEAFix 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.00875)). The other comes from human research on exposure: people shown ideas from many categories produced more diverse ideas, while people shown many ideas from a few categories produced more of the same ([Nijstad et al. 2002](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/914580572)). No study isolates a shared avoid-list as a variable for LLM agents, so the case against the team's list is suggestive, not proven.

What remains in the shared part of the brief should be small and factual. Anthropic's account of its research system says each subagent needs "an objective, an output format, guidance on the tools and sources to use, and clear task boundaries", and reports that early versions without stopping rules kept working after they had enough ([Anthropic engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). A subagent sees nothing of the coordinator's conversation, so any rule that must hold has to be restated in its brief ([Claude Code subagent docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sub-agents.md)).

## Temperature, reasoning effort and model mixing are second-order

Sampling settings matter less than the team might hope. Moving GPT-4o's temperature from 0 to 1 raised diversity from 0.618 to 0.679, and above 1.0 the output was often unstable ([Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553)). The Columbia study saw categories go from 22 to 23 between temperature 1.0 and 1.5, against 28 for people, and garbled text at 2.0 ([Deng et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20408)). Even with temperature 2.0 and a decoding rule that trims unlikely tokens, 61.2% of response pairs stayed above 0.8 similarity ([Jiang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.22954)). A Berkeley design study found no steady effect of temperature or top-p, with temperature 1 and top-p 1 best and prompt changes mattering more than either ([Ma et al. 2024](https://codesign.berkeley.edu/pdfs/papers/ma-llmdiverse-jcise.pdf)). In story generation, temperature is "weakly correlated with novelty" and "moderately correlated with incoherence" ([Peeperkorn et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00492)). The practical rule is to leave temperature at 1.0, never lower it for generating agents, and not spend effort above it.

Reasoning effort is an open question. In one benchmark, more reasoning effort in OpenAI reasoning models tended to reduce both diversity and utility ([Zhang et al., NoveltyBench 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05228)), and on the Wharton scale o3-mini was the least diverse model of all (0.495, fixated on desk organisers) while another reasoning model, DeepSeek R1, was near the top (0.752) ([Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553)). No source measures the thinking modes of current small models.

Small models are not the cause of the collapse. Within a model family, smaller models often give more distinct answers per sample than larger ones, though with lower usefulness scores ([Zhang et al., NoveltyBench 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05228)). Against that, human raters scored ideas from the strongest models as most original ([Liu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.19087)), a search system gained 23% more diversity from a stronger model in the idea-changing role ([Wander 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.00686v1)), and small models copy what they are shown far more readily: asked a question after seeing a wrong majority answer, a 7B model followed the majority 95.3% of the time and a 70B model 9.2% ([Weng et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.13381v2)). Two consequences follow: a small-model fan-out is a reasonable generator, and small generators should be shown as little of each other's output as possible.

Mixing vendors helps, but less than it seems it should. A balanced pool of 25 ideas each from four vendors' models scored **0.763**, above every single model (best 0.756) and statistically indistinguishable from the human pool ([Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553)). Other evidence cuts the other way: responses from different models to open-ended queries are 71% to 82% similar ([Jiang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.22954)); 22 models from several families were collectively far less varied than people ([Wenger & Kenett 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.19361)); and in multi-agent proposal writing, mixing three vendors raised diversity by 34% in one group structure and not at all in another ([Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.18005v2)). Mixing is a cheap addition to the per-agent levers, not a substitute for them. Where a second model family earns its place most clearly is judging, covered below.

Cost does not constrain any of this. At Anthropic list prices read on 19 November 2026, 40 small-model agents that each read a 3,000-token brief and write 2,000 tokens cost roughly $0.05 to $0.52 depending on the Haiku version, before tool use, thinking tokens and retries ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). That is this report's arithmetic, not a published benchmark. Multi-agent systems use about 15 times the tokens of a chat ([Anthropic engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)), but for short ideation briefs the scarce input is the time of the people who choose.

## Three short rounds around a map of the pool beat one large blast

### The human rule "generate alone first" transfers in form, not in cause

Decades of experiments show that people who generate alone and pool afterwards out-produce the same people talking in a room: 18 of 22 experiments favoured the separate individuals, and the main cause was waiting for a turn to speak ([Diehl & Stroebe 1987](https://www.uni-muenster.de/imperia/md/content/psyifp/aeechterhoff/wintersemester2011-12/seminarthemenfelderdersozialpsychologie/08_diehl_stoebe_productivityloss-brainstorming_jpsp1987.pdf)). Parallel agents never wait for a turn, so that cause is absent, and the hidden assumption behind the human result (each person brings different knowledge) fails for copies of one model. The team's one-round design reproduced the form of the human finding without the thing that makes it work.

Three other human findings carry over in substance. What people are shown steers what they produce: exposure to a confederate's typical ideas made people conform to the categories shown and narrowed their breadth ([Kohn & Smith 2011](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/acp.1699)). Alternating solo work with a brief look at others' ideas beat both pure formats on quantity across three experiments, with no clear gain in originality ([Korde & Paulus 2017](https://medium.com/stanford-d-school/alternate-solo-team-work-eb7431893b42)). And selection is its own problem: in a controlled study, the ideas people selected were no better than chance, however the ideas had been generated ([Rietzschel et al. 2006](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/productivity-is-not-enough-a-comparison-of-interactive-and-nomina/)).

### Free discussion between agents narrows the pool

For LLM agents the equivalent of social pressure is conformity to text in context. Across more than 10,000 proposals on 20 topics, "dense communication topologies accelerate premature convergence" and authority suppressed variety: pool diversity (Vendi score, an effective count of distinct items) was 8.08 for flat peer groups, 6.93 for leader-led groups and 4.65 for interdisciplinary expert groups. Judged quality ran the other way, but inside a narrow band (7.88 to 8.50 on a 10-point scale). Going from three to seven interacting agents raised pool diversity by only 7.4%, so diversity per agent more than halved ([Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.18005v2)). This paper is the most relevant single source on agent interaction and it has internal inconsistencies (its results on a blind-writing first phase conflict between main text and appendix), so its numbers are provisional. Other results agree with its direction. In a role-play discussion framework, agents with assigned roles and no discussion scored higher on originality (4.58) than the full discussion (4.44), and discussion without roles scored lowest (3.95) ([Lu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.06373v3)). A 2026 idea-search system found that generating all ideas in one shared context produced no usable distinct ideas by its own measure, and that carrying memory from idea to idea did worse than starting each idea fresh ([IDEAgent 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.22375)). A simulated "hybrid brainstorm", in which GPT-4 merged four sessions and picked the best 10 of 120 ideas, was less diverse than the base prompt (0.393 against 0.377 in similarity) ([Meincke et al. 2024](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/for-web-AI-idea-variance.pdf)). The conclusion for a wide pool is to keep agents from talking freely, to let the coordinator decide what each agent sees between rounds, and to keep "pick the best" out of the generating rounds.

Structured feedback is different. With a small model (GPT-4o-mini), the share of non-duplicate ideas rose from 0.69 in a single pass to 0.77 after one round of critique and revision and to **0.85 to 0.86 after three or four**, with the fourth round adding almost nothing; a persona on the critic gave the best precision among the top ten ideas ([Ueda et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.08350v1)). There was no human evaluation. A scripted contrarian is not supported: a "devil" agent that disagreed by design often lowered accuracy when the first answer was right ([Smit et al. 2024](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/smit24a.html)). Criticism itself is not the problem. In human groups, instructions to debate and even criticise were generally superior to the classic "do not criticise" rule ([Nemeth et al. 2004](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4k70n7v8)). Critique belongs in the process as a separate step with real content, not as a role that objects to everything.

### A grid with one best idea per cell protects coverage

The family of search methods called quality-diversity gives the staging a concrete shape. Its base algorithm, MAP-Elites, lets the user choose the dimensions along which solutions should differ and keeps the single best solution found in each cell of the resulting grid, returning a map instead of one winner ([Mouret & Clune 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.04909)). The versions built on language models use a model three ways: to rewrite an existing text into a new one, to judge quality, and to say which cell a text belongs in. QDAIF used grids of 20 to 100 cells for opinions, stories and poems (for poems, five genres by five tones), and a new text entered the grid if its cell was empty or it beat the occupant ([QDAIF 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.13032)). Rainbow Teaming used a ten-by-ten grid for adversarial prompts and sampled target cells with a bias toward weak ones; in its question-answering test, keeping a grid covered 97% of cells where a baseline that regenerated from scratch each time covered 90% ([Rainbow Teaming 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.16822)). Both found that rewriting without a target leaves cells empty: untargeted rewriting never produced some poem types until a random target genre and tone was put in the prompt ([QDAIF 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.13032)).

Two limits should set expectations. The benefit is coverage, not better ideas on average: in QDAIF's only human evaluation the combined quality-and-diversity score was 0.772 against 0.767 for a fixed few-shot baseline, and average human-rated quality was lower (3.900 against 4.133) ([QDAIF 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.13032)). And variety appears only along the axes someone chose. In one comparison on evolving image prompts, a re-run of the grid method reached half the diversity of a method that simply rewarded distance from everything kept so far (Vendi 1.80 against 3.60) ([Wander 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.00686v1)). A grid therefore needs an "off-grid" bin and a few agents working without a cell. The published text grids imply a budget of roughly 8 to 20 candidates per cell, so a grid of 20 cells needs a few hundred candidates, which is what three rounds of three dozen agents produce. That budget is an inference from the run sizes those papers report.

### Building on parent ideas pays for about three rounds

Evolutionary systems add the idea of producing new candidates from existing ones. The parts that carry over to unscored text are specific. Showing two parents beats one, with diminishing returns beyond two, in program search ([FunSearch 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10794145/)); in an idea system, removing the step that combines the best idea of each cluster with a distant partner cut novelty from 0.40 to 0.25, and removing rank-dependent mutation (gentle edits for the best ideas, radical ones for the worst) cut it to 0.06 ([EvoGens 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.30961v1)). In a system that evolves image prompts, rotating among several named ways of changing a prompt beat using one: diversity was 3.60 with a rotating set, 2.95 with one fixed instruction and 2.61 with none ([Wander 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.00686v1)). Google's AI co-scientist adds variants as new entries that must win their own comparisons ("it doesn't modify or replace existing ones") and appends a running summary of reviewers' recurring objections to later prompts ([Gottweis et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.18864)). A similarity filter before judging matters: without one, a kept set of prompts was far more repetitive (self-BLEU 0.79 against 0.42) ([Rainbow Teaming 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.16822)). Keeping separate sub-populations that exchange only a few items has one clean test at equal budget, on a planning task with an automatic checker, where it raised success from 77.4% to 87.5% ([Mind Evolution 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.09891)). No such test exists for open-ended text.

The headline results of this field (FunSearch, AlphaEvolve) depend on an automatic scorer and thousands to millions of samples. Ideas have no such scorer, and where the judge is a language model, "improvement" means improvement in that judge's opinion. The agreement between AI-judged and human-judged quality dropped in QDAIF's highest score range ([QDAIF 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.13032)). Pressure for novelty alone drifts: one system's outputs left the original concept in about one run in five ([Wander 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.00686v1)), and another, with no minimum quality rule, reports many impractical concepts and outputs nearly twice as long in its most novel run ([Lluminate 2025](https://www.joelsimon.net/lluminate)). The lack of a trustworthy scorer is the likely reason idea systems flatten early. One reports no continued growth in new ideas after three rounds ([Nova 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.14255)); another's diversity went 0.35, 0.53, 0.55 over three rounds ([EvoGens 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.30961v1)). A short run of about three rounds with a stopping rule matches the evidence. A longer run risks teaching the pool to please the judge.

## Models can merge and screen the pile, but people must choose

### Deduplication needs an LLM check on top of embeddings

The usual recipe is sentence embeddings with a cosine cut-off near 0.8. Both studies that popularised that number set it by eye, on different embedding models, and one warns that "changing the embeddings model could yield dramatically different results" ([Meincke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01727)). Embeddings fail in two directions. They split rewordings apart: on 16,294 creativity-test answers, clustering embeddings produced about 588 to 838 "same idea" groups per task where human annotators made 231 to 400, and the best agreement with a human grouping was 0.39 on a scale where two humans reached 0.66 ([Bangash et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16232)). They also place opposites close together: for sentence pairs that humans rated as fairly close paraphrases, a simple negation of the sentence was the nearer one in embedding space 51.57% of the time ([Cao 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.00584)). The design that closed most of the gap uses embeddings only to fetch up to ten similar existing groups and then asks an LLM whether the new idea belongs in one of them or starts a new group; it reached **0.59 against a human annotator**, and originality scores built on its groups tracked human-derived scores more closely than two humans tracked each other ([Bangash et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16232)). That result comes from one task type. A useful test question for "same idea" comes from a diversity benchmark: two outputs are different if a user would benefit from seeing both ([Zhang et al., NoveltyBench 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05228)).

Grouping the deduplicated ideas into themes has the same weakness. The standard clustering options each bring a cost: k-means needs the number of clusters fixed in advance and returns groups of similar size, and agglomerative clustering with a distance cut-off avoids that but is practical only up to a few thousand items ([Sentence-Transformers clustering guide](https://sbert.net/examples/sentence_transformer/applications/clustering/README.html)). Density-based clustering on plain embeddings can get the number of groups badly wrong on short texts: in one benchmark with 77 true categories it estimated 2 ([Lin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06747)). Having a model write and assign theme labels that people can edit matched human-annotated topics better than the strongest conventional baseline (purity 0.74 against 0.64) ([Pham et al., TopicGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01449)). For a pool of a few hundred ideas, a grid defined in advance plus model-written labels checked by people is the more dependable route to themes.

Three details protect rare ideas, which are the point of the exercise. Density-based clustering labels singletons as noise, and one common library function defaults to a minimum group size of 10, which would silently drop every small group in a pool of a few hundred ([Sentence-Transformers reference](https://sbert.net/docs/package_reference/util/retrieval.html)). LLM "same" judgements are not always transitive ([Lin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06747)), so merging by chains of pairwise matches can glue unrelated ideas together; assigning each idea to a group or opening a new one avoids that. And paragraph-length ideas carry style and justification, so the comparison should run on a one- or two-sentence core claim. Idea systems do compress each idea to a short fixed signature before comparing ([IDEAgent 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.22375)), though no study tests the effect on deduplication accuracy.

### LLM judges of ideas agree with experts at about chance

On short, well-defined judgements LLM judges reach human-level agreement: grading chatbot answers, GPT-4 agreed with people 85% of the time where people agreed with each other 81% to 82% ([Zheng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)). On "is this idea new and good" they do not, and studies with current judges show the gap has not closed. The best LLM judge of research ideas reached 53.3% agreement where human reviewers agreed with each other 56.1% of the time and chance is 50% ([Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109)). Against 25,139 ratings from 6,749 scientists, no LLM judge exceeded a correlation of 0.35 on any dimension ([Bao et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08251)). In a blinded test, two experts preferred the human-anchored research question for non-obviousness (78% and 56% of pairs) while two LLM judges preferred the model-written one (82% and 52%), which the authors call a "novelty mirage" ([Sinhahajari et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12071)). Scored against what later happened in the literature, one judge's novelty ratings correlated negatively with outcomes, and judges favoured ambitious, broadly framed ideas over technically specific ones ([Jiang 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15164)). The documented direction of bias is toward ideas that sound impressive. Without a search tool, one system's reviewer rated ideas that had already been published 6.14 out of 10 for novelty, and 2.38 with search ([Gottweis et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.18864)). A model can judge "new compared with this list"; it cannot judge "new to the market" from memory.

Judges are also swayed by surface. Rewriting the better answer in a fearful tone left only 24% to 36% of verdicts unchanged for five of six judges, a statement that most people prefer one answer left 56% to 80% unchanged, and swapping answer order left 57% to 83% unchanged ([Ye et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02736)). The tested counter-measures are plain: rewrite every idea into one neutral format before judging, as the Stanford study did for human and AI ideas alike ([Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109)); judge each pair in both orders and count inconsistent verdicts as a tie ([Zheng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)); and use a judge from a different model family, or a panel. A panel of three small models from different families tracked human rankings better than one large judge (correlation 0.917 against 0.817) at about one-seventh of the cost, though on question answering, not ideas ([Verga et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796)).

### Pairwise comparison inside a theme, never a global league table

Pairwise comparison tracks human judgement better than absolute scoring when items differ in quality ([Liu et al., PairS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.16950)), but it forces a winner between near-equals and amplifies distractions: adding an assertive tone flipped pairwise preferences in about 35% of cases against 9% for absolute scores, and for pairs of equal quality pairwise judging returned a tie under 8% of the time ([Tripathi et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14716)). A brainstorm pool is densest exactly where ideas are near-equal, so comparisons should run inside a theme after deduplication and should allow "no real difference". The best-documented tournament result shows what ranking is good for. A five-round Swiss tournament of pairwise LLM comparisons (ideas with similar running scores are paired each round) separated strong from weak well, yet only 17 of its top 49 ideas matched the 49 a human expert picked from the same pool, and the human-picked set scored higher on every measure ([Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109)). A tournament can discard the bottom of a theme. It cannot pick the shortlist. For stable rankings among human judges each item needs to appear in roughly 10 to 20 comparisons ([Verhavert et al. 2019](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-on-the-reliability-of-comparative-judgement/); [Kinnear et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12246014)); with a single biased judge, more comparisons shrink noise but not bias.

### People eliminate, choose coarsely and protect a few unusual ideas

A single human expert choosing from a generated pool produced a better-rated set than the LLM ranker (5.34 against 4.85 overall) ([Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109)). The cheapest reliable uses of people's time are coarse. Giving evaluators a small budget of tokens to mark the worst ideas filtered a pool more accurately than rating scales in a fraction of the time ([Klein & Garcia 2015](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/114252)), and a variant that also showed voters a wider spread of the idea space halved the pool while keeping 94% of the top ideas ([Lykourentzou et al. 2018](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/when-crowds-give-you-lemons-filtering-innovative-ideas-using-a-di/)). Experts disagree substantially on fine scores for product ideas and agree more on coarse selection ([Hirota et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22517)). More than one person should judge, because one person's grouping has no agreement check.

People also discount novelty. Selecting from their own brainstormed ideas, people showed a "strong tendency to select feasible and desirable ideas, at the cost of originality", and an explicit instruction to select creative ideas changed what they picked ([Rietzschel et al. 2010](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/912990346)). In grant review, a one-standard-deviation rise in novelty lowered a proposal's expected rank by 4.5 percentile points ([Boudreau et al. 2016](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/1/10001229/1/13-053.pdf)). A single averaged score will therefore rank unusual ideas out. Choosing for spread after generating is also the largest diversity effect on record: a simple program that started from one random idea and repeatedly added the idea least similar to those already chosen produced a set of 100 with mean similarity 0.144, far more diverse than any prompt (0.255 at best) or the human pool (0.243), though the paper reports this only in an appendix table ([Meincke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01727)). Judging novelty and feasibility separately, keeping the best idea per cell and reserving a small quota for high-novelty ideas follows from that result and from the quality-diversity work, but no study tests a reserved quota against plain ranking on LLM ideas. The quota is a hedge, and the reason to keep it cheap is the strongest finding in this area: when 43 researchers actually executed ideas, the scores of LLM-generated ideas fell far more than those of human ideas (overall −1.98 against −0.63), erasing their advantage at the idea stage ([Si et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.20803)). A shortlist is a set of bets until something is built or shown to a user.

## Count themes at equal pool size and keep quality on its own line

The literature measures the diversity of an idea set in four ways: mean pairwise embedding similarity; the count or share of unique ideas after deduplication, and how fast new ones dry up as the pool grows; "effective number" measures such as the Vendi score, defined as the exponential of the entropy of the eigenvalues of a similarity matrix ([Friedman & Dieng 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02410)); and coverage of a grid defined in advance. None is validated against human judgements of how diverse an idea set is. The authors of the mean-similarity measure say it "is not clear how it empirically links to human scored measures", that it shifts with language, style and text length, and that "diversity can be obtained by sacrificing idea quality" ([Meincke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01727)). The Columbia group argues that embedding similarity overstates the gap between people and models by roughly two to three times compared with hand-coded categories ([Deng et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20408)); the Wharton group replies that 80% to 90% of their gap survives rewording every idea in a common style ([Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553)). A team should report a hand count of themes beside an embedding number and trust neither alone.

Three rules make sessions comparable. Hold the embedding model, the duplicate cut-off, the idea format and the pool size fixed: two studies that both used a nominal 0.8 cut-off found 95% duplicates in one case and 12% to 14% in the other, because everything else differed ([Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109); [Meincke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01727)). Compare counts at equal pool size by drawing equal-sized random samples from each session, since a larger pool always contains more distinct ideas; this is reasoning from how the measures are defined, not a tested procedure. And do not read much into one run: the 2024 study used at least ten runs per prompt and still warned that some results "might be driven by statistical noise" ([Meincke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01727)).

Quality needs its own line, because every diversity number rises if bad ideas are added. The most useful combined measure in the sources counts the largest set of ideas that all pass a quality bar and are pairwise distinct, so near-identical good ideas count once ([IDEAgent 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.22375)). The team's version of that number is the count of distinct ideas that two or three people marked "keep", and especially the count that fall outside the dozen themes already found. Beyond the session, the only evaluation step shown to change verdicts is execution.

## A ten-step protocol for one coordinator and three dozen agents

The protocol assumes about 36 generating agents per round on small models, a coordinator on a strong model, a checking and judging model from a different family where one is available, and two or three people. The Claude Code default of 20 concurrent subagents means each round runs in two waves ([Claude Code subagent docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sub-agents.md)). No published experiment tests this whole sequence against the team's baseline; it is assembled from separate studies with different tasks, models and measures. Three terms are used throughout. A **card** is one idea in a fixed short format. A **ticket** is the part of a brief that differs between agents. A **bucket** is a group of cards judged to be the same idea. The specific numbers (36 agents, 15 titles, 6 cards, 20 cells) are this report's choices within the ranges the sources use, not tested optima. By this report's own arithmetic the whole run is a few thousand mostly short model calls, which at the list prices cited earlier costs dollars, not hundreds of dollars. The binding constraint is the time of the people in Steps 1, 8 and 9.

| Step | Who | What happens | Strength of evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | People, coordinator | Fix the measures; re-score the two old sessions | Cautions well documented; procedure inferred |
| 2 | Coordinator, people | Short core brief; grid, personas, constraints, lenses | One direct study plus adjacent evidence |
| 3 | Coordinator | A different ticket per agent; models and settings | Two independent groups agree; one counter-result |
| 4 | 36 agents, plus a control arm | Round 1: blind generation, draft then diversify | Two independent groups agree; gain is modest |
| 5 | Checking model | Normalise, deduplicate, bucket, map onto the grid | One study on a different task |
| 6 | 36 agents | Round 2: refill empty and thin cells from parents | Targeting well supported; operators untested on product ideas |
| 7 | Critics, authors | Round 3: critique, repair, stop | Several systems agree; all judged by models |
| 8 | Judging model | Screen inside buckets and cells | Weakness of judges well documented; fixes partly tested |
| 9 | Two or three people | Eliminate, choose a portfolio, reserve unusual ideas | Human studies, some read as abstracts; quota untested |
| 10 | People | Probe the survivors; fill the scorecard | One strong execution study; scorecard inferred |

### Step 1: Fix the measures and re-score the two old sessions

**Do.** Define the card: title of eight words or fewer, one-sentence mechanism, target user, situation, the assumption it breaks, and what would have to be true for it to work. Rewrite the output of both old sessions into cards. Choose one embedding model and keep it for good. Hand-label 50 to 100 pairs of cards, spread across the similarity range, as "same idea" or "different" and set the duplicate cut-off from them. Have two people independently count themes on equal-sized random samples from each old session. Record unique share, buckets per 100 cards and mean pairwise similarity as the baseline.

**Rests on.** Cut-offs are set by eye in every source and do not transfer between embedding models ([Meincke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01727)); embedding and category measures can differ by a factor of two to three ([Deng et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20408)); human reviewers of ideas agree only 56% to 61% of the time, so one coder is not enough ([Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109); [Bao et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08251)).

**Weak or missing.** No source validates a cut-off or any embedding diversity measure against human judgement of idea sets. The size of the labelling sample is a judgement.

### Step 2: Write a short core brief and map the space

**Do.** Cut the shared brief to about 150 words: the goal, the audience, limits that are truly fixed, the card format and a stopping rule. Remove example ideas and advice on how to be creative. Rewrite the dozen known themes as one-line abstract labels headed "already covered". Have the coordinator model propose, and people edit, a grid of two axes with four or five named values each (16 to 25 cells), choosing axes the old brief never varied, for example who the idea is for and how it works. Also prepare about 30 ordinary personas spread across the user axis, about a dozen hard constraints of the "extreme but possible" kind, six to eight lenses (contradiction-resolving, assumption reversal, far analogy and others) and a few truly remote source domains.

**Rests on.** A planning call that splits the space into directions gave the largest gain in its study at 1.61 times the tokens ([Ibrahim et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.30150)); published text grids are small, categorical and hand-designed ([QDAIF 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.13032); [Rainbow Teaming 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.16822)); examples and creativity articles anchor ([Meincke et al. 2024](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/for-web-AI-idea-variance.pdf)); category labels beat example ideas as a way to show what is covered ([IDEAFix 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.00875); [Nijstad et al. 2002](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/914580572)).

**Weak or missing.** No experiment compares a grid with an open brief for LLM ideation, and none says how many axes or cells are best. Axes proposed by a model are untested; QDAIF's authors list them as future work. Variety will appear only along the chosen axes. "Too much shared context causes convergence" is supported indirectly, through example anchoring, and has not been tested on a long brief.

### Step 3: Give every agent a different ticket, and set models and sampling

**Do.** Give about 28 agents a ticket with one grid cell, one ordinary persona from that cell's user group, and either one hard constraint or one lens, never both. Cover every cell at least once and let no two agents share both a cell and a lens or constraint. Give the remaining eight or so agents no cell: these work from a remote source domain or an extreme constraint and exist to find what the grid cannot. If two or three small-model families are available, spread them evenly across cells so that model and cell are not confounded. Leave temperature at 1.0 where it can be set. Do not add reasoning effort to generating agents without testing it.

**Rests on.** Per-session ordinary personas (0.757) and constraints (0.735) against 0.679, with ordinary beating exotic ([Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553)); the same persona result on hand-coded categories from a second group ([Deng et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20408)); one persona per prompt, not several ([Feng et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/dsj.2025.10037)); the inverted U for constraints ([Acar et al., HBR 2019](https://hbr.org/2019/11/why-constraints-are-good-for-innovation)); temperature as a minor lever in four independent studies ([Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553); [Deng et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20408); [Ma et al. 2024](https://codesign.berkeley.edu/pdfs/papers/ma-llmdiverse-jcise.pdf); [Jiang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.22954)); the four-vendor pool at 0.763.

**Weak or missing.** One dataset shows personas reducing diversity. No study measures personas or constraints on small models beyond direction, none tests a persona, a cell and a constraint stacked together, and none tests the four-vendor pool with small models or at this scale. Most gains are in embedding distance; whether the added ideas are good was checked by people in very few studies.

### Step 4: Round 1, blind generation with a draft-then-diversify routine

**Do.** Agents see nothing of each other. Each brief follows the template below. Each agent writes its cards to its own file and returns only the path and the count. The coordinator checks every file against the card format, the ticket, and the presence of both the draft list and the revised list, and re-runs failures once. In the same round, run a control arm: the same number of agents on the old brief, with the same models and card format, so the two designs can be compared at equal pool size.

```
GOAL (same for every agent): two or three sentences.
HARD LIMITS: only limits that are truly fixed.
ALREADY COVERED: theme labels, one line each, no example ideas.

YOUR TICKET (different for every agent)
  Cell: {who it is for} x {how it works}
  You are: {an ordinary person from that group, two sentences}
  Constraint or lens: {exactly one}

DO THIS
  1. List 15 one-line idea titles that fit your ticket.
  2. Revise the list: no two alike, none under ALREADY COVERED,
     replace the most obvious ones. Unconventional ideas are welcome.
  3. Write up the 6 least obvious as cards.
  4. Stop. If your cell seems empty, say so and do not pad.

CARD: agent_id, cell, constraint_or_lens, title (8 words or fewer),
  mechanism (one sentence), user, situation, assumption_broken,
  what_must_be_true
Write the draft list, the revised list and the cards to {path}.
Return only the path and the number of cards.
```

**Rests on.** Many ideas per call against one (0.679 against 0.452) ([Terwiesch et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.27553)); the draft, revise, describe routine in two groups' studies, and its combination with personas as the top condition ([Meincke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01727); [Deng et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20408)); the 15% of runs that skipped the revision; the brief contents and file hand-off from Anthropic's research system ([Anthropic engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)); and the finding that disobeying the task specification and missing stop conditions are among the commonest multi-agent failures, at 11.8% and 12.4% of annotated failures ([Cemri et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3)).

**Weak or missing.** The routine's gain shrank from large in 2024 to 0.019 on GPT-4o in 2026. The tested version drafted 100 titles; 15 and 6 are scaled down for small models without a test. Whether small models carry out the revision faithfully is unknown, which is why the check exists.

### Step 5: Merge the pool: normalise, deduplicate, bucket and map

**Do.** Have the checking model rewrite each card's core claim as one or two neutral sentences of similar length, with no agent names. Embed the core claims. For each card, fetch the five to ten nearest existing buckets and ask the checking model: "Is this the same idea as any of these? Would the team gain from seeing both?" Place the card in one bucket or open a new one. Never merge buckets through chains of pairwise matches, and keep every singleton. Label each bucket with its grid cell or "off-grid". Produce a coverage map: which cells are empty, thin (one bucket) or crowded, plus a list of bucket labels. People spot-check 20 bucket and cell assignments by hand.

**Rests on.** Embedding retrieval plus an LLM bucket decision reached 0.59 agreement with a human annotator against 0.39 for embedding clustering and 0.66 between humans ([Bangash et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16232)); embeddings confuse negations with paraphrases ([Cao 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.00584)); style normalisation before judging ([Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109)); similarity filters before judging keep a kept set varied ([Rainbow Teaming 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.16822)).

**Weak or missing.** The bucket method was tested only on short creativity-test answers. Reducing ideas to a core claim before embedding is untested. QDAIF's abstract reports "reasonable agreement" between human and AI ratings without a figure ([QDAIF abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13032)), so how well a model assigns product ideas to cells is unknown.

### Step 6: Round 2, send agents to empty and thin cells with parents and a named operator

**Do.** Each agent receives the core brief; a target cell, empty and thin cells first; one or two parent cards from the pool; one operator from a rotating list; and the labels of crowded buckets as "already covered". The operators are: combine these two (the second parent drawn from a distant bucket); reverse the parent's central assumption; carry the parent's mechanism into the target cell; shrink it to its smallest useful version; move away from these three crowded labels. No agent sees the whole pool. Agents use the same draft-and-revise routine and return three or four cards. New cards join the pool and parents stay. Keep six to eight agents off-grid as before. Re-run Step 5 afterwards.

**Rests on.** Targeted rewriting fills cells that untargeted rewriting leaves empty ([QDAIF 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.13032)); sampling toward weak cells ([Rainbow Teaming 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.16822)); combination with a distant partner ([EvoGens 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.30961v1)); rotating operators beat one ([Wander 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.00686v1)); add, never replace ([Gottweis et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.18864)); dense sharing speeds convergence and small models conform most ([Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.18005v2); [Weng et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.13381v2)).

**Weak or missing.** No source compares operators on product ideas, and in the one study that looked, which operator did best differed by domain with no correlation across domains ([Lluminate 2025](https://www.joelsimon.net/lluminate)). "Two parents beat one" comes from code. A category map against a flat avoid-list has not been tested head to head for LLM agents. Splitting agents into separate groups that exchange a few ideas is a possible extension, but its only clean test is on a scored planning task.

### Step 7: Round 3, critique, repair and stop

**Do.** For one representative of each bucket, a critic agent on a different model family, briefed as a sceptical expert (an engineer on the team's product, a tired user), writes the strongest objection and what would have to be true. An author agent then writes one repaired variant as a new card. Allow at most one repair and one refinement per idea. Fill cells that are still empty with a last targeted batch. Keep a running one-line list of rejected patterns and recurring objections and append it to briefs. Re-run Step 5. Stop after this round, or after Round 2 if fewer than one new card in ten opened a new bucket.

**Rests on.** Critique-and-revision rounds raised the non-duplicate share from 0.69 to 0.86 with gains ending at the third or fourth round, and a persona on the critic gave the best precision ([Ueda et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.08350v1)); a critic step with written feedback was the most important component of a small-model evolution system on a scored task ([Mind Evolution 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.09891)); a limit of one repair and two refinements, with 28 of 30 repairs succeeding ([IDEAgent 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.22375)); saturation at about three rounds ([Nova 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.14255); [EvoGens 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.30961v1)).

**Weak or missing.** The stopping rule is inferred from saturation curves; no source validates one for unscored text. All the supporting gains are judged by models or embeddings. Longer discussion raised novelty and lowered workability in one study ([Chen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.04575)), so repairs may trade one for the other.

### Step 8: Screen by machine inside buckets and cells, not across the pool

**Do.** First test the judge. Two or three people label 50 to 100 normalised cards as keep, maybe or drop. If the judging model agrees with the people about as well as they agree with each other, use it for the bulk; if not, confine it to deduplication. The judge is a different model family from the generators, or a panel of three small models from different families. It answers two separate questions on normalised cards. Is this card on-brief and coherent? Within a crowded bucket or cell, which of these two is the stronger version, asked in both orders with "no real difference" allowed and inconsistent answers counted as ties? Record novelty and feasibility as separate coarse ratings and never average them. Use the output only to choose each bucket's representative and to drop the clearly broken.

**Rests on.** LLM judges of ideas at or below human-human agreement and biased toward impressive framing ([Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109); [Bao et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08251); [Jiang 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15164)); position, tone and majority effects with their counter-measures ([Ye et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02736); [Zheng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)); the panel result ([Verga et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796)); forced winners between near-equals ([Tripathi et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14716)); a statistical procedure for deciding whether an LLM can stand in for human annotators from a modest labelled sample ([Calderon et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.10970)).

**Weak or missing.** No study measures LLM judges on product ideas against a small product team's own judgement. Panel evidence is from question answering. The figures on comparisons per item come from human judges.

### Step 9: People choose a portfolio and reserve places for unusual ideas

**Do.** Two or three people work independently through the grid: one representative per bucket, plus every singleton and off-grid card. On the first pass each person marks the clear losers with a fixed budget of marks. On the second pass each marks keep or maybe on what remains, under an explicit instruction to choose at least a few ideas for originality regardless of feasibility. The portfolio is the best surviving idea in each cell plus a quota of three to five unusual ideas; ideas on which people disagree strongly go to the quota and not to the bin. Check novelty against the market by search or first-hand knowledge.

**Rests on.** Eliminating the worst is faster and more accurate than rating everything, and a diversity-aware version kept 94% of top ideas while halving the pool ([Klein & Garcia 2015](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/114252); [Lykourentzou et al. 2018](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/when-crowds-give-you-lemons-filtering-innovative-ideas-using-a-di/)); experts agree more on coarse selection ([Hirota et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22517)); people select against originality unless told otherwise ([Rietzschel et al. 2010](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/912990346)); a human pick beat the LLM ranker ([Si et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109)); novelty judged without search is inflated ([Gottweis et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.18864)).

**Weak or missing.** No study tests a reserved quota or a rule based on disagreement, and none compares rating representatives with rating every idea. The elimination studies were read as abstracts, and a later one finds that accuracy depends on the size of the budget of marks ([Lukumon & Klein 2023](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/decisn/v50y2023i2d10.1007_s40622-023-00349-w.html)). Isolated ideas are not usually the most valuable ones ([Kornish & Ulrich 2011](https://repository.upenn.edu/fnce_papers/104)), so the quota should stay small.

### Step 10: Probe the survivors and fill the scorecard

**Do.** Give each portfolio idea a cheap test before believing any ranking: a mock-up, a one-day build, or five conversations with users. Then fill one scorecard for the new design and one for the control arm, at equal pool size by repeated random sampling: buckets per 100 cards; unique share at the fixed cut-off; cells filled; themes counted by two people on equal samples; keeps and maybes per 100 cards; keeps that fall outside the dozen old themes, which is the main result; cost and minutes of human time. Change one thing at a time in later sessions.

**Rests on.** Execution reversed the idea-stage advantage of LLM ideas ([Si et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.20803)); single runs are noisy ([Meincke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01727)); counting only ideas that are both good and mutually distinct ([IDEAgent 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.22375)).

**Weak or missing.** One run per design does not prove a difference; resampling shows only whether the gap exceeds variation within the runs. No source reports results for this set-up, so the team's control arm will be the only direct evidence it has.

## Conclusion

The research changes what a mass brainstorm is. A few dozen copies of a model are a single source sampled many times, and their variety has to be designed in beforehand through the differences between their briefs. The coordinator's main work therefore moves to before the agents run (mapping the space and writing tickets) and to between rounds (showing each agent a map and a target, not a pile). Generation is nearly free at current prices. Judgement is the scarce resource, and no model supplies it yet, so the right size for a session is set by how many buckets two or three people can read carefully, not by how many agents can run.

The same research cannot say whether the wider pool will contain better ideas. Model ideas cluster where ideas are commercially plausible, the most distant ideas are not usually the most valuable, and ideas rated novel on paper lose most when executed. The control arm, the fixed scorecard and the count of kept ideas outside the dozen known themes will start to answer that question for the team's own product, which the literature cannot do. Each later session that changes one thing adds to that answer.
